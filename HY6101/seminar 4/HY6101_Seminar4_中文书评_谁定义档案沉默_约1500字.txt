Who Defines “Silence”?
After reading the articles by Michael Moss and David Thomas, Iyra S. Buenrostro, and Swapan Chakravorty, and reflecting on our class discussion, my strongest impression was not simply that archives contain gaps. Rather, I began to ask: who has the authority to define an absence as silence, and who decides which voices are worth preserving, interpreting, and circulating? Drawing on Trouillot’s discussion of the production of history, these articles led me to see archives not as transparent containers of the past, but as processes shaped by record-makers, institutions, governments, technologies, and researchers. My basic view is that silence may result from power and political suppression, but it may also arise from limited resources, mistakes in classification, the limits of a medium, and individual choices. Historians should therefore not simply try to “fill” silences. Instead, they should ask when, where, and through what mechanisms a silence was produced, and why it has become audible only today.
“Absence of evidence does not mean evidence of absence” is the first important lesson I take from these readings. Historical research often asks us to identify gaps, but this expectation may also lead researchers to quickly claim that they have discovered a suppressed group. A source may simply be uncatalogued, held by another institution, written in another language, or excluded by the researcher’s own search terms. This has made me reconsider confirmation bias: when researchers enter an archive with a fixed assumption, they may notice only materials that support their argument. At the same time, I do not believe that all silences are merely imagined by researchers. Censorship, fear, and inequality can genuinely prevent records from being created. In my view, silence has a historical basis, but it must also be recognized by later researchers. These two dimensions cannot be separated.
I particularly agree with the idea that silence is not necessarily negative. Making more records public may help victims gain recognition, but it does not always produce reconciliation. Public records may also create stigma, harm descendants, or be used by new political narratives. An individual’s unwillingness to discuss trauma may be a form of self-protection rather than a problem that scholars need to correct. What is most troubling is not the existence of silence itself, but the fact that it may remain unrecognized or be presented as a complete and natural history. This has led me to reconsider my earlier belief that silence can be completely avoided. Silences caused by poor management, restricted access, and unreasonable institutional systems can be reduced, but selectivity is unavoidable as long as research requires the selection of questions, sources, and cases.
Regarding power and inequality, I no longer accept the single explanation that the state simply silences marginalized groups. Marginalized groups often experience the greatest lack of representation, but political losers, local communities, and people in intermediate social positions may also be excluded. Traditional orthodox histories in China provide a useful example. They often describe the Western Regions and northern nomadic societies from the perspective of the Central Plains dynasties, with an emphasis on war, conquest, and tribute relations. As a result, the political logic and everyday experiences of these groups become less visible. Groups without their own writing systems may face greater archival difficulties, but the absence of written records cannot be equated with the absence of history. The key question is whether later historians are willing to treat oral traditions, archaeology, material culture, and sources in other languages as meaningful evidence. Non-state archives are not automatically more accurate. Families, corporations, media organizations, and academic institutions can also act as gatekeepers. Non-state actors may even marginalize official narratives through powerful external interpretations. It is therefore more useful to compare different forms and consequences of power than simply to choose one side.
Before this course, I mainly saw the positive aspects of digitization in historical research. It clearly improves research efficiency and, to some extent, reduces barriers to access. However, digitization also makes the question of who defines meaning more concrete. It improves preservation and remote access, but it is impossible to digitize all archives at the same time. Should priority be given to fragile materials, local newspapers, minority languages, or materials that are frequently used or capable of challenging established narratives? These standards are shaped by governments, libraries, funding bodies, platforms, and researchers. If only national newspapers are digitized, local experiences will remain silent. If only wealthy universities can subscribe to databases, digitization may deepen inequality in access to research resources. AI may further amplify this bias because it most easily absorbs materials that are already digitized and searchable. My view is that digitization should be treated as a starting point, not as a replacement for original documents, catalogues, or fieldwork. Databases should also make their selection criteria, coverage, and omissions clear. I have also begun to realize that the significance of an archive does not depend on whether it is used immediately. Unused records may still reshape future research questions. Preservation should therefore not be based only on market demand; it should also consider whether it gives neglected groups opportunities to narrate their own histories.
Photographs and oral history have also made me reconsider the question of authenticity. Photographs can supplement textual archives and help people recall the emotions, relationships, and bodily experiences connected to an event. When combined with oral history, they may allow researchers to hear tones of voice and aural details that written records cannot provide. However, photographs are not inherently truthful. Framing, purpose, preservation, and later viewing can all change their meaning, while memory can be reshaped by time, trauma, and the interview process. The interviewer is not only a collector and classifier but also someone who selects material through questions. Oral archives therefore contain both voices and silences from the moment they are created. For this reason, methods should follow the research question rather than the novelty of a tool. Visual analysis, oral history, archaeology, linguistics, and digital humanities can be used to check one another. The key issue is to explain what each method makes visible and what it continues to hide.
The value of these articles lies in bringing archival politics, media forms, and research ethics into the same discussion. The point that convinces me most is that archives are “activated” through every act of searching, interpreting, and reusing them; history is therefore not a fixed product. At the same time, the readings leave an important question unresolved: when we advocate democratic and alternative archives, how can we establish transparent standards for digitization, representation, and privacy without replacing state-centered bias with a romantic view of “popular voices”? In my future research, the important question is not how to claim that the complete past has been recovered. Instead, I want to ask: under what institutional and media conditions does a voice become visible? When I try to make one voice heard, whose silence might I create in the process? This also requires me to preserve my research trail and unresolved questions rather than presenting only a polished final conclusion.
