# Reading Documents through Their Creation: A Review of Elizabeth Yale’s “The History of Archives”

Elizabeth Yale’s “The History of Archives: The State of the Discipline” explains why archives are not neutral storage places. Her main point is clear in the sentence “No archive is innocent” (Yale 2015, 332). Their contents depend on who created records, what was kept, and who controlled access. Yale starts with Derrida and Foucault and then discusses material practices such as filing, storage, and access (334–36). She covers early modern states, empire, postcolonial justice, gender, and personal archives. The many cases can be dense, but together they connect archives with power, historical writing, and memory.

Yale shows that power shapes both the creation and survival of records. Jean-Baptiste Colbert gathered reports to help govern France, but his archive was a private collection inherited by his son. When political responsibilities changed, it lost much of its function (338–39). An archive that appears to represent the state may therefore depend on one person’s position and interests. The line between a public archive and a private one is not always clear.

The most useful part of Yale’s article for my own research is her discussion of how documents were created. Yale quotes Kathryn Burns’s description of archives as “less like mirrors than like chessboards” (340). A document is not simply a reflection of an event. It can also be a move made by someone who wants a legal, political, or social result. We therefore need to ask who wrote it, what form they had to follow, what they wanted to achieve, and who was expected to read it. This point is especially important when I use newspapers. A page of *Shenbao* is not a single voice. An official notice may have been written by a government office, selected and reprinted by newspaper editors, and placed beside comments from scholars or other contributors. Each part of the page has a different creator and purpose.

In my research on the history of Chinese medicine in modern China, I found a discussion about an infant’s death that was connected to an allegedly improper dosage of Chinese medicine. The official concern focused on whether the clinic was operating legally and how medicine should be regulated. The Social Bureau introduced rules calling for instructions on how Chinese medicines should be used. However, the discussion in the newspaper did not remain at the level of law and administration. Some people questioned the doctor’s medical ability, while others doubted whether standard instructions could fit the practice of Chinese medicine. Different doctors might give different prescriptions for patients with the same illness because they judged each patient’s condition differently. Some contributors therefore suggested explaining the effects and qualities of each medicine instead of fixing one standard prescription. There were also claims that some doctors had special methods for treating serious diseases such as cancer and changed their prescriptions according to each patient. Strict standardization might make such treatment more difficult. The disagreement was not only about whether regulation was good or bad. It was also about who had the right to define safe treatment and what kinds of medical knowledge could be put into a standard written form.

Thinking about the creation of these texts changes how I read this newspaper material. If I read only the official regulation, I may conclude that the main problem was illegal practice or unclear dosage. If I read only the comments, I may miss the government’s need to reduce risk and make medical work easier to manage. I should separate the different layers on the newspaper page: the original official text, the editor’s choice and arrangement, the opinions of named or unnamed contributors, and the digital database through which I now read them. I also need to consider the intended audience. A government office wrote its notice to regulate medical practice, while a newspaper also had to inform readers, attract attention, and provide space for debate. At the same time, not every member of society could publish an opinion in *Shenbao*. Editors decided what appeared, and literacy and social position affected who could join the discussion. For this reason, the newspaper is valuable for comparing official and social views, but it cannot be treated as a complete record of public opinion.

Yale notes that women and other nonelites often entered official records only when they came into contact with courts or other institutions (348–50). The history of women subjected to Japanese military sexual slavery, often called “comfort women,” has similar problems. Few records were created or preserved, and some survivors did not wish to speak because of social pressure and personal pain. Historians can combine military, legal, and civilian sources with careful oral history, but they should not fill every silence with speculation.

Archives can show both power and weakness. In Ann Laura Stoler’s study, Dutch colonial officials had authority but acted with incomplete knowledge and anxiety (343). The *Shenbao* case is similar. The rules show government power, while the need for them and the doubts around them reveal uncertainty about medical skill, dosage, and individual difference.

Yale reviews a field rather than studying one archive. Her eighty-two notes bring together history, archival science, book history, literary studies, and anthropology. The examples make her argument convincing, although the quick movement between them can be hard to follow. Her main contribution is to show that checking whether a document is authentic is only the beginning. Before using a document as evidence, I should ask: Who created it, for what purpose and audience, and what later decisions allowed it to survive and reach me? This helps me read both the information in a source and the process that shaped it.
