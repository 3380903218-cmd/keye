How Memories Become Records 
HY6101 Seminar 11 & 12 | Reading Reflection on Moments of Silence 
While reading Moments of Silence, I kept wondering: does silence in memory necessarily 
lead to silence in history? Experiences that a person has not spoken about may still remain in 
photographs, documents or other people's memories. Yet even when these materials exist, they 
may not enter public discussion. I am therefore more concerned with the conditions under 
which personal experiences can be recorded, and with what may prevent them from being 
recorded. Thongchai's discussion of “unforgetting” makes me want to understand more clearly 
how memories become records. Many choices in this process are subjective: what different 
people care about and what they are willing to say will affect the records that are left behind. 
In class, the teacher distinguished between silence about historical facts and silence about 
their implications. This distinction made me think that knowing an event happened does not 
necessarily mean being willing to acknowledge what it means. The 1996 commemoration 
discussed in the book allowed more public discussion of the massacre and its violence. But 
discussion of who carried out the violence, and why, remained restricted.1 Commemoration 
opened up space for discussion, but how far could people go? Acknowledging that people 
suffered and acknowledging who was responsible are different things. How much the state 
acknowledges, and where it remains silent, will affect how the event is remembered later. 
The “chronopolitics” of memory left a strong impression on me. I think time both changes 
and shapes memory. When people's circumstances and perspectives change, they may 
understand the same experience differently, even without new evidence. But I think political 
events already affect memory when it first forms. Social events draw attention to certain things, 
make some details memorable and shape people's attitudes. The accounts broadcast before the 
massacre had even ended were already providing a framework for later collective memory.2 
The role of public media and social media also matters to me. For people who did not take 
part in an event and knew little about it, reporting and the spread of information can direct 
their attention to particular things. This affects how they later understand and remember the 
event.

Thinking about this, I find it hard to see collective memory as simply a collection of 
personal experiences. Political forces and social conditions also affect how individuals 
understand, recall and describe their experiences. I do not think a personal memory is 
“meaningless” just because it falls outside mainstream concerns. But I also accept that once it 
is recognized as part of collective memory, it is more easily recorded and used. This is also 
where the difficulty lies: if personal and collective memories focus on different things, how 
should archives record them? Must an experience be described in familiar terms to make it 
easier to understand? If this changes what the person originally wanted to express, some of 
what they meant may still go unheard, even if a record exists. 
I am also wondering how official records describe events that ordinary people remember 
but find difficult to discuss in public. The amnesty bill discussed in the book was influenced 
by the massacre, yet its wording avoided mentioning the massacre itself. This stands out to 
me. The names given to events can also reflect different attitudes. Calling the October 1973 
uprising the “Day of Extreme Grief” emphasized violence and death, while calling it the “Day 
of Extreme Joy” emphasized the political outcome.3 I therefore think we should ask more than 
whether official documents exist. We also need to examine what the documents included and 
how they described it. Could what official accounts avoid or leave out also affect ordinary 
people's memories and their accounts of experiences that are difficult to express? I have not 
yet worked out an answer to this question. 
On the other hand, when the truth about the past is unclear, missing records are not always 
the reason. Sometimes the relevant records exist, but there are problems with how they are 
organized, identified and connected. This was the case with the identification of Jaruphong's 
body in the book. The autopsy reports and photographs existed, but pages and photographs 
from several reports were in the wrong order, which misled those trying to identify him.4 This 
makes me think that a historian's perspective also affects research. Whether historians connect 
two sources, or think a detail deserves further investigation, affects what can eventually be 
known. If people later continue to rely on incorrect identifications, categories or descriptions, 
these will also affect how the experiences of those involved are understood.

hard to find a common focus. Also, how will the historian record the answers? Choosing what

very different answers. 
This also makes me wonder: are the memories of ordinary people and historians affected 
differently by social and personal factors? Historians have methods for checking and 
comparing sources, but I do not think this allows them to step completely outside their own 
circumstances. Thongchai is both a participant and a researcher. He can gain access to certain 
memories, but at times he does not want to understand his former opponents more deeply. He 
writes about these limits, but I still want to ask: if historians' questions and records are shaped 
by their own perspectives, how can they notice choices they do not realize they are making? 
This makes me pay more attention to the difference between what an interviewee says and 
what the historian finally writes down. 
I think some “silences” do not want to remain silent. Many unspoken experiences are 
waiting to be expressed and recorded. Efforts to express these silences through 
commemoration, collecting materials and telling stories also leave new records and create new 
historical voices. But when I think about those involved, I find it hard to say that more 
complete records and more public information are always better. In the book, a father's account 
of his search for his son has no ending. When Thongchai asked the father to continue writing, 
he realized that the request might cause unbearable pain.6 For a researcher, this is an unfinished 
source. For the father, however, the lack of an ending may be linked to a hope he does not 
want to give up. What the researcher wants to find out may therefore be something the person 
involved is unwilling to discuss further.

My concern is whether a researcher's wish to preserve the truth could lead them to 
underestimate this pain. In class, a classmate reflected that telling people who had suffered 
from war to “move on” could silence them, even if it was meant kindly. I think this connects 
with my concern. Asking someone to speak, or asking them to stop speaking, may fail to

longer asking questions about the event, questions about responsibility may continue to be 
avoided. This is also something I find difficult to deal with. 
I still cannot say whether archival work should always aim to make as much of the truth 
about the past public as possible. But I still think that a personal memory does not become 
meaningless simply because it has not gained collective recognition. Nor does a person's 
silence mean that this past has been forgotten. How do the perspectives of those making 
records and the choices of those involved affect the history that is left behind? This is a 
question I want to keep thinking about.
