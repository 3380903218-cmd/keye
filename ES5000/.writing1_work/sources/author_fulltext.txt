L26: As a social species, humans rely on a safe, secure social surround to survive and thrive. Perceptions of social isolation, or loneliness, increase vigilance for threat and heighten feelings of vulnerability while also raising the desire to reconnect. Implicit hypervigilance for social threat alters psychological processes that influence physiological functioning, diminish sleep quality, and increase morbidity and mortality.
L27: The purpose of this paper is to review the features and consequences of loneliness within a comprehensive theoretical framework that informs interventions to reduce loneliness. We review physical and mental health consequences of loneliness, mechanisms for its effects, and effectiveness of extant interventions. Features of a loneliness regulatory loop are employed to explain cognitive, behavioral, and physiological consequences of loneliness and to discuss interventions to reduce loneliness.
L28: Loneliness is not simply being alone. Interventions to reduce loneliness and its health consequences may need to take into account its attentional, confirmatory, and memorial biases as well as its social and behavioral effects.

L100: Introduction
L101: 
L102: Loneliness is a common experience; as many as 80% of
L103: 
L104: those under 18 years of age and 40% of adults over
L105: 65 years of age report being lonely at least sometimes [1–
L106: 
L107: 3], with levels of loneliness gradually diminishing through
L108: 
L109: the middle adult years, and then increasing in old age (i.e.,
L110: 
L111: ≥70 years) [2]. Loneliness is synonymous with perceived
L112: 
L113: social isolation, not with objective social isolation. People
L114: 
L115: can live relatively solitary lives and not feel lonely, and
L116: 
L117: conversely, they can live an ostensibly rich social life and
L118: 
L119: feel lonely nevertheless. Loneliness is defined as a
L120: distressing feeling that accompanies the perception that
L121: 
L122: one’s social needs are not being met by the quantity or
L123: 
L124: especially the quality of one’s social relationships [2,4–6].
L125: 
L126: Loneliness is typically measured by asking individuals to
L127: 
L128: respond to items such as those on the frequently used
L129: 
L130: UCLA Loneliness Scale [7]: “I feel isolated,”“There are
L131: 
L132: people I can talk to,”and “I feel part of a group of friends.”
L133: 
L134: The result is a continuum of scores that range from highly
L135: 
L136: socially connected to highly lonely.
L137: Each of us is capable of feeling lonely, and loneliness
L138: 
L139: is an equal opportunity tenant for good reason. We have
L140: 
L141: posited that loneliness is the social equivalent of physical
L142: 
L143: pain, hunger, and thirst; the pain of social disconnection
L144: 
L145: and the hunger and thirst for social connection motivate
L146: 
L147: the maintenance and formation of social connections
L148: 
L149: necessary for the survival of our genes [8,9]. Feelings of
L150: 
L151: loneliness generally succeed in motivating connection or
L152: reconnection with others follow ing geographic relocation
L153: 
L154: or bereavement, for instance, thereby diminishing or
L155: 
L156: abolishing feelings of social isolation. For as many as
L157: 
L158: 15–30% of the general population, however, loneliness is
L159: 
L160: a chronic state [10,11]. Left untended, loneliness has
L161: 
L162: serious consequences for cognition, emotion, behavior,

L200: Loneliness Matters for Physical Health and Mortality
L201: 
L202: A growing body of longitudinal research indicates that
L203: 
L204: loneliness predicts increased morbidity and mortality [12–
L205: 
L206: 19]. The effects of loneliness seem to accrue over time to
L207: 
L208: accelerate physiological aging [20]. For instance, loneliness
L209: 
L210: has been shown to exhibit a dose–response relationship
L211: with cardiovascular health risk in young adulthood [12].
L212: 
L213: The greater the number of measurement occasions at which
L214: 
L215: participants were lonely (i.e., childhood, adolescence, and
L216: 
L217: at 26 years of age), the greater their number of cardiovas-
L218: 
L219: cular health risks (i.e., BMI, systolic blood pressure (SBP),
L220: 
L221: total, and HDL cholesterol levels, glycated hemoglobin
L222: 
L223: concentration, maximum oxygen consumption). Similarly,
L224: 
L225: loneliness was associated with increased systolic blood
L226: pressure in a population-based sample of middle-aged
L227: 
L228: adults [21], and a follow-up study of these same individuals
L229: 
L230: showed that a persistent trait-like aspect of loneliness
L231: 
L232: accelerated the rate of blood pressure increase over a
L233: 
L234: 4-year follow-up period [22]. Loneliness accrual effects are
L235: 
L236: also evident in a study of mortality in the Health and
L237: 
L238: Retirement Study; all-cause mortality over a 4-year follow-
L239: 
L240: up was predicted by loneliness, and the effect was greater in
L241: chronically than situationally lonely adults [17]. Penninx et
L242: 
L243: al. [15] showed that loneliness predicted all-cause mortality
L244: 
L245: during a 29-month follow-up after controlling for age, sex,
L246: 
L247: chronic diseases, alcohol use, smoking, self-rated health,
L248: 
L249: and functional limitations. Sugisawa et al. [18] also found a
L250: 
L251: significant effect of loneliness on mortality over a 3-year
L252: 
L253: period, and this effect was explained by chronic diseases,
L254: 
L255: functional status, and self-rated health. Among women in
L256: the National Health and Nutrition Survey, chronic high
L257: 
L258: frequency loneliness (>3 days/week at each of two
L259: 
L260: measurement occasions about 8 years apart) was prospec-
L261: 
L262: tively associated with incident coronary heart disease
L263: 
L264: (CHD) over a 19-year follow-up in analyses that adjusted
L265: 
L266: for age, race, socioeconomic status, marital status, and
L267: 
L268: cardiovascular risk factors [19]. Depressive symptoms have
L269: 
L270: been associated with loneliness and with adverse health
L271: outcomes, but loneliness continued to predict CHD in these
L272: 
L273: women after also controlling for depressive symptoms.
L274: 
L275: Finally, loneliness has also been shown to increase risk for
L276: 
L277: cardiovascular mortality; individuals who reported often
L278: 
L279: being lonely exhibited significantly greater risk than those
L280: 
L281: who reported never being lonely [14]. In sum, feelings of
L282: 
L283: loneliness mark increased risk for morbidity and mortality,
L284: 
L285: a phenomenon that arguably reflects the social essence of
L286: 
L287: our species.
L288: Loneliness Matters for Mental Health and Cognitive
L289: 
L290: Functioning
L291: 
L292: The impact of loneliness on cognition was assessed in a
L293: 
L294: recent review of the literature [9]. Perhaps, the most striking
L295: 
L296: finding in this literature is the breadth of emotional and
L297: 
L298: cognitive processes and outcomes that seem susceptible to
L299: 
L300: the influence of loneliness. Loneliness has been associated
L301: 
L302: with personality disorders and psychoses [23–25], suicide
L303: 
L304: [26], impaired cognitive performance and cognitive decline
L305: over time [27–29], increased risk of Alzheimer’s Disease
L306: 
L307: [29], diminished executive control [30,31], and increases
L308: 
L309: in depressive symptoms [32–35]. The causal nature of the
L310: 
L311: association between loneliness and depressive symptoms
L312: 
L313: appears to be reciprocal [32], but more recent analyses of
L314: 
L315: five consecutive annual assessments of loneliness and
L316: 
L317: depressive symptoms have shown that loneliness predicts
L318: 
L319: increases in depressive symptoms over 1-year intervals, but
L320: depressive symptoms do not predict increases in loneliness
L321: 
L322: over those same intervals [36]. In addition, experimental
L323: 
L324: evidence, in which feelings of loneliness (and social
L325: 
L326: connectedness) were hypnotically induced, indicates that
L327: 
L328: loneliness not only increases depressive symptoms but also
L329: 
L330: increases perceived stress, f ear of negative evaluation,
L331: 
L332: anxiety, and anger, and diminishes optimism and self-esteem
L333: 
L334: [8]. These data suggest that a perceived sense of social
L335: connectedness serves as a scaffold for the self—damage the
L336: 
L337: scaffold and the rest of the self begins to crumble.
L338: 
L339: A particularly devastating consequence of feeling social-
L340: 
L341: ly isolated is cognitive decline and dementia. Feelings of
L342: 
L343: loneliness at age 79 predicted “lifetime cognitive change”
L344: 
L345: as indicated by lower IQ at age 79 adjusting for IQ at age
L346: 
L347: 11, living arrangements at age 11 and at age 79, sex, marital
L348: 
L349: status, and ideal level of social support [27]. This finding
L350: does not rule out a reverse causal direction; cognitive
L351: 
L352: impairments may hamper social interactions, prompt social
L353: 
L354: withdrawal, and thus lead to loneliness. Other studies,
L355: 
L356: however, have indicated that loneliness is a precursor of
L357: 
L358: cognitive decline. For instance, the cognitive functioning of
L359: 
L360: 75–85-year-olds (as assessed by the Mini-Mental State
L361: 
L362: Examination) did not differ as a function of loneliness at
L363: 
L364: baseline but diminished to a greater extent among those
L365: high than low in loneliness over a 10-year follow-up [28].
L366: 
L367: In a prospective study by Wilson et al. [29], loneliness was
L368: 
L369: inversely associated with performance on a battery of
L370: 
L371: cognitive measures in a sample of 823 initially dementia-
L372: 
L373: free older adults. Moreover, loneliness at baseline was
L374: 
L375: associated with a faster decline in cognitive performance on
L376: 
L377: most of these measures over a 4-year follow-up. This was
L378: 
L379: ann. behav. med. (2010) 40:218–227 219
L380: not true of the converse: cognitive status at baseline did not
L381: 
L382: predict changes in loneliness. In addition, incidence of
L383: 
L384: Alzheimer’s disease (76 individuals) was predicted by
L385: 
L386: degree of baseline loneliness after adjusting for age, sex,
L387: 
L388: and education; those in the top decile of loneliness scores
L389: 
L390: were 2.1 times as likely to develop Alzheimer’s disease
L391: 
L392: than those in the bottom decile of loneliness scores.
L393: 
L394: Depressive symptoms had a modest effect on Alzheimer’s
L395: disease risk, but loneliness continued to exert a significant and
L396: 
L397: much larger influence on Alzheimer’s disease than depressive
L398: 
L399: symptoms when depressive symptoms were included in the
L400: 
L401: model [29]. Overall, it appears that something about our
L402: 
L403: sense of connectedness with others penetrates the physical
L404: 
L405: organism and compromises the integrity of physical and
L406: 
L407: mental health and well-being. What that “something”might
L408: 
L409: be is the topic to which we next turn.
L410: 
L411: How Loneliness Matters: Mechanisms
L412: The Loneliness Model Our model of loneliness [8,9] posits
L413: 
L414: that perceived social isolation is tantamount to feeling
L415: 
L416: unsafe, and this sets off implicit hypervigilance for
L417: 
L418: (additional) social threat in the environment. Unconscious
L419: 
L420: surveillance for social threat produces cognitive biases:
L421: 
L422: relative to nonlonely people, lonely individuals see the
L423: 
L424: social world as a more threatening place, expect more
L425: 
L426: negative social interactions, and remember more negative
L427: social information. Negative social expectations tend to
L428: 
L429: elicit behaviors from others that confirm the lonely persons'
L430: 
L431: expectations, thereby setting in motion a self-fulfilling
L432: 
L433: prophecy in which lonely people actively distance them-
L434: 
L435: selves from would-be social partners even as they believe
L436: 
L437: that the cause of the social distance is attributable to others
L438: 
L439: and is beyond their own control [37]. This self-reinforcing
L440: 
L441: loneliness loop is accompanied by feelings of hostility,
L442: stress, pessimism, anxiety, and low self-esteem [8] and
L443: 
L444: represents a dispositional tendency that activates neurobi-
L445: 
L446: ological and behavioral mechanisms that contribute to
L447: 
L448: adverse health outcomes.
L449: 
L450: Health behaviors One of the consequences of loneliness
L451: 
L452: and implicit vigilance for social threat is a diminished
L453: 
L454: capacity for self-regulation. The ability to regulate one’s
L455: 
L456: thoughts, feelings, and behavior is critical to accomplish
L457: 
L458: personal goals or to comply with social norms. Feeling
L459: socially isolated impairs the capacity to self-regulate, and
L460: 
L461: these effects are so automatic as to seem outside of
L462: 
L463: awareness. In a dichotic listening task, for instance, right-
L464: 
L465: handed individuals quickly and automatically attend pref-
L466: 
L467: erentially to the pre-potent right ear. Latency to respond to
L468: 
L469: stimuli presented to the non-dominant ear can be enhanced,
L470: 
L471: however, by instructing participants to attend to their left
L472: 
L473: ear. Among young adults who were administered this task,
L474: the lonely and nonlonely groups did not differ in perfor-
L475: 
L476: mance when directed to attend to their pre-potent right ear,
L477: 
L478: but the lonely group performed significantly worse than the
L479: 
L480: nonlonely group when directed to shift attention to their
L481: 
L482: non-prepotent left ear [30]. In other words, automatic
L483: 
L484: attentional processes may be unimpaired, but effortful
L485: 
L486: attentional processes are compromised in lonely relative to
L487: 
L488: socially connected individuals.
L489: 
L490: Of relevance for health is the capacity for self-regulation
L491: in the arena of lifestyle behaviors. Regulation of emotion
L492: 
L493: can enhance the ability to regulate other self-control
L494: 
L495: behaviors [38], as is evident from research showing that
L496: 
L497: positive affect predicts increased physical activity [39]. In
L498: 
L499: middle-aged and older adults, greater loneliness was
L500: 
L501: associated with less effort applied to the maintenance and
L502: 
L503: optimization of positive emotions [31]. Compromised
L504: 
L505: regulation of emotion in lonely individuals explained their
L506: diminished likelihood of performing any physical activity,
L507: 
L508: and loneliness also predicted a decrease in physical activity
L509: 
L510: over time [31]. Physical activity is a well-known protective
L511: 
L512: factor for physical health, mental health, and cognitive
L513: 
L514: functioning [40], suggesting that poorer self-regulation may
L515: 
L516: contribute to the greater health risk associated with
L517: 
L518: loneliness via diminished likelihood of engaging in
L519: 
L520: health-promoting behaviors. A related literature shows that
L521: loneliness is also a risk factor for obesity [41] and health-
L522: 
L523: compromising behavior, including a greater propensity to
L524: 
L525: abuse alcohol [42]. To the extent that self-regulation
L526: 
L527: accounts for poorer health behaviors in lonely people,
L528: 
L529: better health behaviors may be more easily accomplished in
L530: 
L531: the actual or perceived company of others. Interestingly,
L532: 
L533: animal research has shown that social isolation dampens the
L534: 
L535: beneficial effects of exercise on neurogenesis [43], imply-
L536: ing that health behaviors may better serve their purpose or
L537: 
L538: have greater effect among those who feel socially
L539: 
L540: connected than those who feel lonely. This hypothesis
L541: 
L542: remains to be tested, but research on the restorative effects
L543: 
L544: of sleep is consistent with this notion.
L545: 
L546: Sleep Countering the physiological effects of the challenge
L547: 
L548: of daily emotional, cognitive, and behavioral experiences,
L549: 
L550: sleep offers physiological restoration. Experimental sleep
L551: deprivation has adverse effects on cardiovascular function-
L552: 
L553: ing, inflammatory status, and metabolic risk factors [44]. In
L554: 
L555: addition, short sleep duration has been associated with risk
L556: 
L557: for hypertension [45], incident coronary artery calcification
L558: 
L559: [46], and mortality [47].
L560: 
L561: What is less appreciated is that sleep quality may also be
L562: 
L563: important in accomplishing sleep’s restorative effects.
L564: 
L565: Nonrestorative sleep (i.e., sleep that is non-refreshing
L566: 
L567: despite normal sleep duration) results in daytime impair-
L568: ments such as physical and intellectual fatigue, role
L569: 
L570: 220 ann. behav. med. (2010) 40:218–227
L571: 
L572: impairments, and cognitive and memory problems [48]. We
L573: 
L574: have noted that loneliness heightens feelings of vulnerabil-
L575: 
L576: ity and unconscious vigilance for social threat, implicit
L577: 
L578: cognitions that are antithetical to relaxation and sound
L579: 
L580: sleep. Indeed, loneliness and poor quality social relation-
L581: 
L582: ships have been associated with self-reported poor sleep
L583: 
L584: quality and daytime dysfunction (i.e., low energy, fatigue),
L585: but not with sleep duration [49–52]. In young adults,
L586: 
L587: greater daytime dysfunction, a marker of poor sleep quality,
L588: 
L589: was accompanied by more nightly micro-awakenings, an
L590: 
L591: objective index of sleep continuity obtained from Sleep-
L592: 
L593: Caps worn by participants during one night in the hospital
L594: 
L595: and seven nights in their own beds at home [53]. The
L596: 
L597: conjunction of daytime dysfunction and micro-awakenings
L598: 
L599: is consistent with polysomnography studies showing a
L600: conjunction, essentially an equivalence, between subjective
L601: 
L602: sleep quality and sleep continuity [54], and substantiates
L603: 
L604: the hypothesis that loneliness impairs sleep quality.
L605: 
L606: In an extension of these findings, loneliness was associated
L607: 
L608: with greater daytime dysfunction in a 3-day diary study of
L609: 
L610: middle-age adults, an association that was independent of age,
L611: 
L612: gender, race/ethnicity, household income, health behaviors,
L613: 
L614: BMI, chronic health conditions, daily illness symptom severity,
L615: and related feelings of stress, hos tility, poor social support, and
L616: 
L617: depressive symptoms. Cross-lagged panel analyses of the three
L618: 
L619: consecutive days indicated potentially reciprocal causal roles
L620: 
L621: for loneliness and daytime dysfunction: lonely feelings
L622: 
L623: predicted daytime dysfunction the following day, and daytime
L624: 
L625: dysfunction exerted a small but significant effect on lonely
L626: 
L627: feelings the following day [55], effects that were independent
L628: 
L629: of sleep duration. In other words, the same amount of sleep is
L630: less salubrious in individuals w ho feel more socially isolated
L631: 
L632: and, ironically, less salubrious sleep feeds forward to further
L633: 
L634: exacerbate feelings of social isolation. This recursive loop
L635: 
L636: operates outside of consciousness and speaks to the relative
L637: 
L638: impenetrability of loneliness to intervention.
L639: 
L640: Physiological functioning The association between loneli-
L641: 
L642: ness and cardiovascular disease and mortality [13,14,19]
L643: 
L644: may have its roots in physiological changes that begin early
L645: in life. As noted earlier, chronic social isolation, rejection,
L646: 
L647: and/or feelings of loneliness in early childhood, adoles-
L648: 
L649: cence, and young adulthood cumulated in a dose–response
L650: 
L651: fashion to predict cardiovascular health risk factors in
L652: 
L653: young adulthood (26 years old), including elevated blood
L654: 
L655: pressure [12]. In our study of young adults, loneliness was
L656: 
L657: associated with elevated levels of total peripheral resistance
L658: 
L659: (TPR [49,56]). TPR is the primary determinant of SBP
L660: until at least 50 years of age [57], which suggests that
L661: 
L662: loneliness-related elevations in TPR in early to middle-
L663: 
L664: adulthood may lead to higher blood pressure in middle and
L665: 
L666: older age. Consistent with this hypothesis, loneliness was
L667: 
L668: associated with elevated SBP in an elderly convenience
L669: 
L670: sample [49], and in a population-based sample of 50–68-
L671: 
L672: year-old adults in the Chicago Health, Aging, and Social
L673: 
L674: Relations Study [21]. The association between loneliness and
L675: elevated SBP was exaggerated in older relative to younger
L676: 
L677: lonely adults in this sample [21], suggesting an accelerated
L678: 
L679: physiological decline in lonely relative to nonlonely indi-
L680: 
L681: viduals. Our recent study of loneliness and SBP in these
L682: 
L683: same individuals over five annual assessments supported this
L684: 
L685: hypothesis. Short-term (i.e., 1 year) fluctuations in loneliness
L686: 
L687: were not significant predictors of SBP changes over 1-year
L688: 
L689: intervals, but a trait-like component of loneliness present at
L690: study onset contributed to greater increases in SBP over 2-,
L691: 
L692: 3-, and 4-year intervals [22]. These increases were cumula-
L693: 
L694: tive such that higher initial levels of loneliness were
L695: 
L696: associated with greater increases in SBP over a 4-year
L697: 
L698: period. The prospective effect of loneliness on SBP was
L699: 
L700: independent of age, gender, race/ethnicity, cardiovascular
L701: 
L702: risk factors, medications, health conditions, and the effects of
L703: 
L704: depressive symptoms, social support, perceived stress, and
L705: hostility [22]. Elevated SBP is a well-known risk factor for
L706: 
L707: chronic cardiovascular disease, and these data suggest that
L708: 
L709: the effects of loneliness accrue to accelerate movement along
L710: 
L711: a trajectory toward serious health consequences [20].
L712: 
L713: The physiological determinants responsible for the cumu-
L714: 
L715: lative effect of loneliness on blood pressure have yet to be
L716: 
L717: elucidated. TPR plays a critical role in determining SBP in
L718:
