## Abstract

Background: Loneliness is detrimental to mental health, with university students at higher risk of feeling lonely than other population groups. However, little research has explored interventions to reduce loneliness among students. This review identifies the characteristics and effectiveness of interventions targeting university/college students.

Methods: PsycINFO, Medline, ASSIA and Web of Science were searched from inception using keywords linked to 'loneliness', 'intervention' and 'students'. Relevant peer and nonpeer-reviewed English-language articles on studies implementing an intervention with loneliness as an outcome and investigating undergraduate or postgraduate students at a higher education institution were included for quality analysis and narrative synthesis. Risk of bias was assessed at both study level and at outcome level.

Results: Twenty-eight articles were included, comprising 25 quantitative and three qualitative studies, covering 37 interventions, most implemented in the United States. Interventions were based on psychoeducation, social support groups, increasing social interaction or reflective exercises. The age of the participants (n = 2339) ranged from 17.62 to 25 (mean age 20.63) years. Evidence from the RCTs suggests that most interventions influenced loneliness outcomes, but the magnitude of the benefit is unclear. Across quantitative studies, 80% (16/20) of interventions based on either social support groups, increasing social interaction or reflective exercises, and 50% (7/14) of interventions based on psychoeducation were deemed effective in reducing loneliness. Most interventions measured quantitatively were delivered in a group setting, of which two thirds were considered effective in reducing loneliness scores, regardless of intervention.

Conclusions: Universities have a choice of interventions to help reduce loneliness among students either on campus or virtually. Ones promoting social connectedness appear to be more successful. More high-quality studies in a larger number of countries are needed, taking vulnerable student groups into consideration.

Keywords: Students; college; interventions; loneliness; mental health; prevention; social connectedness; university.

The first three of these latest reviews included a small number of studies on undergraduate students (Bruehlman-Senecal et al., 2020; Loucks et al., 2021; Mattanah et al., 2010; Zhang, Fan, et al., 2018), but contained no special focus or recommendations regarding the university setting. Williams et al. (2021) recognised students as a specific population group but, due to concentrating on interventions compatible with COVID-19 shielding and social distancing measures, identified only five studies focusing on students, all interventions involving a psychological or cognitive component (Conoley & Garber, 1985; Dowd et al., 2014; McWhirter & Horan, 1996; Seepersad, 2005; Zhang, Fan, et al., 2018).

University students are in a transitional phase; they often live away from home for the first time in their lives, and have various academic, financial and social pressures and concerns, which sets them apart from many adults (Horgan, McCarthy, & Sweeney, 2013) and other young people (Lim et al., 2020). Whilst students may benefit from interventions identified in the earlier reviews, it is unclear, which are most suitable and effective for students in a university setting. Most previous reviews have contained interventions explicitly targeting loneliness even though there may be broader, ‘indirect’ approaches to well-being that do not specifically aim to address loneliness but have important impacts on loneliness (Mann et al., 2017). Due to the scarcity of studies targeting students, in this narrative systematic review our aim was to identify, describe and analyse the effectiveness of intervention studies addressing loneliness in university students (either directly or indirectly). The research questions were: (a) What are the characteristics of interventions that address levels of loneliness among students in higher education (university)? (b) What is the evidence for the effectiveness of interventions that alleviate loneliness among university students?

#### Study findings

Evidence from the RCTs suggests that most interventions had an effect on loneliness outcomes, but the magnitude of the benefit is unclear (see Table S4). These included six group-delivered psychoeducation interventions (5, 6, 8, 9, 15, 21), five reflective exercises (3, 13, 14, 20, 22), one support group intervention (4) and four social interaction interventions (2, 12, 17, 19). Controlled pre–post (CPP) and pre–post (PP) studies also suggest that group-delivered psychoeducation (41), support group (38) and social interaction (31, 33, 34) may be effective in reducing loneliness. Table S4 reports pre and post mean scores and differences in mean scores (post–pre) to aid estimation of direction and size of effect; studies have been grouped based on study design and [version of] loneliness measure to aid comparison of scores. The largest reduction in UCLA3 (20 item) loneliness score was observed with group delivered psychoeducation (22) but this may reflect the fact that the study targeted those most lonely. A social interaction intervention involving group-based basketball three times a week resulted in a large reduction in the ULS-8 (UCLA 8 item) loneliness score (12).

Across all study types, 20 out of 28 group interventions reduced levels of loneliness (1, 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 12, 13, 14, 15, 17, 19, 21, 22, 26, 28, 31, 32, 33, 34). Three out of six individual interventions (performed with or without the use of the internet) reduced loneliness scores, two in the reflection category (27, 20). Of the interventions performed online or digitally, three were effective in reducing loneliness scores regardless of whether they were performed in an online group or individually (19, 20, 31). Two of these interventions involved chatting to another online (19, 31), and the other involved typing journal entries (20).

Seven of 14 psychoeducation interventions were found to be effective (5, 6, 8, 9, 15, 21, 28) as were three of four social support interventions (4, 26, 32), and seven of nine reflective exercise interventions (1, 3, 13, 14, 22, 27, 29). Three reflective exercises utilised meditation and mindfulness (3, 22, 27), two promoted mindful movements (13, 14), one utilised writing in a journal (20) and one promoted a mindful lifestyle (1). Six of seven social interaction interventions reduced loneliness scores (12, 17, 19, 31, 33, 34), one of which utilised nonhuman interaction with a dog and facilitator (34). Most of the interventions that were not deemed to be effective were in the psychoeducation category, of which, five of eight were studied in 1996 or earlier (7,8, 9, 10, 11). It should be noted that the majority of this evidence came from RCTs (7/12).

Studies involving students with pre-existing mental health problems or feelings of loneliness (diagnosed or self-declared) used a range of interventions including social interaction (2), reflective exercise (22), social support (26) and psychoeducation interventions (5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 16, 18, 21). However, only six interventions targeting these students significantly reduced loneliness score (5, 6, 8, 9, 21, 22).

Although no study directly compared the effect of a single intervention across subgroups, reductions in feelings of loneliness appeared consistent across groups. For example, reductions in loneliness were seen across students of all educational disciplines, in those with autism spectrum disorder (32) and in students from varied geographic locations. Similarly, reductions in feelings of loneliness were reported in studies sampling mostly female students (8, 9) and in studies sampling mostly male students (32).

### Strength and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review to explore interventions addressing loneliness in university students. Following the original submission of this systematic review, two systematic reviews of interventions aimed at reducing loneliness in young people have been published (Eccles & Qualter, 2021; Osborn et al., 2021). However, neither draw special attention to university students.

The scope of our search included younger university students, from the age of 17, to graduate university students. As there is some overlap in age with college students (aged 16–18 years in the UK), our findings may also be applicable to those in postsecondary educational settings.

Our search strategy incorporated a range of search terms, multiple databases and was not limited by the date of publication. Two independent reviewers completed both screening and quality assessment of all research ensuring reliability. Whilst previous systematic reviews have focused on interventions with loneliness as the primary outcome, our review included studies with loneliness as either primary or secondary outcome, thus capturing all relevant research in this area and drawing attention to interventions, which may serve multiple purposes.

Interventions were classified under broad groupings, between which will be some overlap as most appeared to include components of multiple categories. Interventions were, therefore, categorised based on their most prominent theme introducing an element of subjectivity.

### Implications for future research

The majority of research identified was deemed at an increased risk of bias due to quasi-experimental study designs. Further studies adopting a randomised controlled trial design are needed, especially among students with elevated loneliness or mental health problems. Further research is also needed to compare the effect of a given intervention across multiple subgroups, and to evaluate the effectiveness of an intervention longitudinally. Due to the lack of research in BAME students and those with autism or disability, further experimental studies should be conducted in these subgroups. Evidence demonstrates these groups are particularly vulnerable to feelings of loneliness (Kennedy & Barker, 2020; Reed, Giles, Gavin, Carter, & Osborne, 2016). ## Ethical information

This is a systematic review. No ethical approval or written consent were required.

# Fight the Epidemic of Loneliness One Relationship at a Time

## Peer friendships are vital to college mental health.

Posted August 26, 2025 | Reviewed by Margaret Foley

### Key points

  * College relationships are critical, but that doesn’t mean it’s easy to make and keep friends.
  * Feelings of homesickness or self-doubt can cause students to withdraw just when they need to reach out.
  * Making meaningful friendships and being a part of a close community takes intention over time.

Relationship issues are tied with stress/anxiety as the most discussed issue at college counseling centers. Students who don’t feel close to their friends express a palpable amount of pain.

No one needs a Ph.D. in psychology to know that good relationships are important, but there is a ton of research that shows how essential good relationships are to mental health and even to physical health and longevity. Studies show that life feels more meaningful when we are connected and feel we belong. We are hardwired for bonding, yet it can be hard to connect in meaningful ways, which is something many students struggle with in college.

## Be Open and Nonjudgmental

In order to create a new community of friends at college, it's often necessary to go outside of one's comfort zone. Be open to new situations, and don’t judge if it takes a long time to make new friends. Making deep, intimate connections with new people takes time and shared experience. Know that what feels hard at first will get easier. Be patient.

## Keep Making the Effort

Not everyone we meet will become a good friend. Try new strategies and situations, and keep putting in the time and energy to form new friendships. When we keep going out, showing up, and making ourselves available, we become more comfortable connecting with others.

## Go Where the People Are

It’s great when students find themselves in a ready-made group, like a sports team, an amazing class, or that lucky dorm floor. But without a ready-made group, it becomes important to be creative. Pick the places where others hang out. It’s simple and logical, but it may not feel natural at first. It can feel uncomfortable to show up in places when we don’t yet know people, whether that’s a lounge, the library, a particular eatery, or a park. Our natural inclination is to play it safe, but showing up is half the battle. Arrive early to class so there is extra time to chat with other students. Study in the same area in the library, and the regulars will become recognizable. Being in the same place, it’s easier to start a conversation about whatever is going on. So hang out where others hang out.

## Reach Out

When friend prospects have been identified, make the effort to reach out. Contact them and make a plan to do something. It doesn’t have to be involved or special. Relate it to the context of the friendship, such as getting together to study for a class or meeting for lunch once a week when schedules match. Reach out more than once, even if that feels hard and out of balance. --------------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Fitting in versus belonging: “Relation-less relationships”

Most well-intended colleges and universities strive to solve the problem of social isolation with their students, without addressing the underlying loneliness. This is analogous to treating a migraine headache with an aspirin, focused on the symptom rather than the source.

The source, it seems, lies largely with the quality of student relationships.

THE BASICS

  * Understanding Loneliness
  * Take our Loneliness Test
  * Find a therapist near me

Since the pandemic we have managed (to some degree) to coerce students out of their dorm rooms, but the root cause of loneliness is going to take more of a concerted effort to approach. This means that we will need to make a fundamental shift from helping students to fit in—to helping them acquire a genuine sense of belonging.

Teenagers and young adults will do nearly anything to avoid the excruciating pain of social rejection. Helping students to understand that fitting in is about acquiring external approval, which is often at the expense of altering ourselves in some way, can be accomplished with interactive discussions and activities. I know that with my own students, most were quite aware that chronically seeking external approval is not only exhausting, but it also inevitably takes a toll on one's self-esteem. This often perpetuates the problem of loneliness, as damaged self-esteem can circle back around to social isolation.

On the other hand, genuine belonging is about knowing our own value. When students know their own value, they do not feel the need to change and adapt who they are to meet the needs of the group. They also don't feel the need to isolate. When students find their people in this way, it’s authentic. True belonging is a deep and meaningful connection. This is what our students need most, and we can help them get there by mirroring their value with every given opportunity.
