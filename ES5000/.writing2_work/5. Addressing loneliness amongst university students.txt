Review: Interventions addressing loneliness amongst
university students: a systematic review
Olivia Betty Ellard, Christina Dennison & Helena Tuomainen
Warwick Medical School, University of Warwick, Coventry, UK
Background: Loneliness is detrimental to mental health, with university students at higher risk of feeling
lonely than other population groups. However, little research has explored interventions to reduce loneliness
among students. This review identiﬁes the characteristics and effectiveness of interventions targeting univer-
sity/college students. Methods: PsycINFO, Medline, ASSIA and Web of Science were searched from inception
using keywords linked to ‘loneliness’, ‘intervention’ and ‘students’. Relevant peer and nonpeer-reviewed
English-language articles on studies implementing an intervention with loneliness as an outcome and investi-
gating undergraduate or postgraduate students at a higher education institution were included for quality
analysis and narrative synthesis. Risk of bias was assessed at both study level and at outcome level. Results:
Twenty-eight articles were included, comprising 25 quantitative and three qualitative studies, covering 37
interventions, most implemented in the United States. Interventions were based on psychoeducation, social
support groups, increasing social interaction or reﬂective exercises. The age of the participants ( n = 2339) ran-
ged from 17.62 to 25 (mean age 20.63) years. Evidence from the RCTs suggests that most interventions inﬂu-
enced loneliness outcomes, but the magnitude of the beneﬁt is unclear. Across quantitative studies, 80% (16/
20) of interventions based on either social support groups, increasing social interaction or reﬂective exercises,
and 50% (7/14) of interventions based on psychoeducation were deemed effective in reducing loneliness. Most
interventions measured quantitatively were delivered in a group setting, of which two thirds were considered
effective in reducing loneliness scores, regardless of intervention. Conclusions: Universities have a choice of
interventions to help reduce loneliness among students either on campus or virtually. Ones promoting social
connectedness appear to be more successful. More high-quality studies in a larger number of countries are
needed, taking vulnerable student groups into consideration.
Key Practitioner Message
 Young people often feel lonely on a regular basis. This is a risk factor for the development of mental health
conditions. Mental health services provided by universities and other settings do not meet the demand for
support, a factor in declining mental health of young people.
 Interventions that bring students together around an activity, in a support group or to socialise, either in
person or virtually, can reduce feelings of loneliness in young people. Meditation or mindfulness may bene-
ﬁt those who prefer not to join groups.
 Universities have the power to tackle loneliness, a major health concern in this population. Practitioners
need to be involved in designing, facilitating and maintaining safe spaces and opportunities for students
and young people to interact and bond to prevent this signiﬁcant risk factor evolving into mental health
diagnoses in the future.
Keywords: Students; university; college; mental health; loneliness; social connectedness; interventions; prevention
Background
University students have been found to suffer with
depression, stress and anxiety (Chen et al., 2013; Dalky
& Gharaibeh, 2019; Stallman, 2010; Thorley, 2017).
This is being addressed globally by improvements to uni-
versity mental health services (Warwick, Maxwell, Sta-
tham, Aggleton, & Simon, 2008). Despite this, college
students in the United States (US) reporting past-year
treatment (therapy/counselling and/or psychotropic
medication) for mental health concerns increased from
19% to 34% between 2007 and 2017 (Lipson, Lattie, &
Eisenberg, 2018). In the United Kingdom (UK), the num-
ber of ﬁrst-year students disclosing a mental health
condition has increased almost ﬁvefold (from 0.4% in
2006 to 2% in 2016), and there has been a signi ﬁcant
rise in the demand for student support services; in some
universities, up to 25% of students are using, or waiting
to use, counselling services (Thorley, 2017). Meeting
this increasing demand is a challenge (Holm-Hadulla
& Koutsoukou-Argyraki, 2015; Hughes, Panjwani,
Tulcidas, & Byrom, 2018; Thorley, 2017; Williams
et al., 2015). New approaches are needed to improve stu-
dent wellbeing. A preventative strategy is to focus on stu-
dent loneliness.
Loneliness is a subjective, negative experience associ-
ated with the sense of having fewer social contacts than
desired and/or a lack of anticipated level of intimacy in

relationships (Yanguas, Pinazo-Henandis, & Tarazona-
Santabalbina, 2018). Loneliness is associated with
greater anxiety, stress and depression in students
(Diehl, Jansen, Ishchanova, & Hilger-Kolb, 2018; Mann
et al., 2022; Richardson, Elliott, & Roberts, 2017) and
has been associated with maladaptive coping styles,
sleep problems, lower life satisfaction and suicidal idea-
tion (Kurina et al., 2011; Zhang, Fan, Huang, & Rodri-
guez, 2018). Loneliness has also been associated with
impaired cognitive health (Lim, Eres, & Vasan, 2020).
Loneliness impacts brain functionality, with chronic
loneliness changing the nature and likelihood of social
interactions (Lim et al., 2020).
In 2014–15, 32.4% of students in Germany felt moder-
ately lonely and 3.2% severely lonely (Diehl et al., 2018).
In 2018, young people aged 18 –22 were identi ﬁed as the
loneliest generation of Americans (Demarinis, 2020).
The same year, 9.8% of young people in the UK aged 16 –
24 years ‘often’ felt lonely (Snape et al., 2018). A follow-
on survey found that over 15% of students reported feel-
ing lonely on a day-to-day basis, with ﬁgures worse for
Black, Asian and Minority Ethnic (BAME), disabled and
international students, and those living at home
(WONKHE, 2019). In 2020, students across the globe
were affected by the global COVID-19 pandemic. This
caused the closure of campuses, the restriction of face-
to-face teaching and the cancelling of sports and society
events. Students were con ﬁned to their accommodation
or at home with parents. Opportunities to develop
friendships with other students who share interests and
hobbies were reduced (Buote et al., 2007). During the
ﬁrst lockdown, young people and students in the UK and
U.S. experienced loneliness and suffered from psycho-
logical distress more intensely than the general popula-
tion (McGinty, Presskreischer, Han, & Barry, 2020).
Many reported experiencing mental health symptoms
(Tang et al., 2022). In the UK, 18 –24 year olds were
nearly three times more likely than 65 –74 year olds to
have experienced loneliness ‘more often than normal ’
(Royal Society for Public Health, 2020).
The evidence suggests a greater need for interventions
to reduce loneliness in students, both in response to
restrictions related to the COVID-19 pandemic but also
outside of such restrictions. While charities and health
services offer help for those feeling lonely at university,
targeted interventions have been developed to attempt to
relieve loneliness. Discussion groups allow students to
talk with others about the stresses of university, rela-
tionships and self-identity (Gold, Bentzley, Franciscus,
Forte, & De Golia, 2019). Social skills training enable
students to overcome loneliness by learning new skills
(Orchard, 1986). Online chat rooms and forums allow
students to connect with other students from home
(Barber, 2011). Although universities currently offer var-
ious relevant interventions that address student loneli-
ness, these have not been evaluated thoroughly or tested
among larger population groups. There is a need for a
solid research base (Hunt & Eisenberg, 2010).
Masi, Chen, Hawkley, and Cacioppo ( 2011) grouped
loneliness interventions aimed at adults into four cate-
gories: improving social skills, enhancing social support,
increasing opportunities for social contact and address-
ing maladaptive social cognition. Maladaptive social cog-
nition can result in, for example, negative evaluations of
other people and lack of interpersonal trust. Addressing
maladaptive social cognition was found to be the most
effective; however, students were not identi ﬁed as a sub-
group. Bessaha et al. ( 2020) discovered that interven-
tions involving technology and support groups
signiﬁcantly reduced loneliness in various adult groups.
Again, students were not identiﬁed as a subgroup.
Whilst reviews by Eccles and Qualter ( 2021) and
Osborn, Weatherburn, and French ( 2021) targeted
young people, they focused on a wide age range, 3 –25
and 10 –25 years, respectively, and a variety of settings.
Hickin et al. ( 2021) reviewed participants between the
ages of 8 –81 years, and Williams et al. ( 2021) included
studies with no speci ﬁed age range. The ﬁrst three of
these latest reviews included a small number of studies
on undergraduate students (Bruehlman-Senecal
et al., 2020; Loucks et al., 2021; Mattanah et al., 2010;
Zhang, Fan, et al., 2018), but contained no special focus
or recommendations regarding the university setting.
Williams et al. ( 2021) recognised students as a speci ﬁc
population group but, due to concentrating on interven-
tions compatible with COVID-19 shielding and social
distancing measures, identi ﬁed only ﬁve studies focus-
ing on students, all interventions involving a psychologi-
cal or cognitive component (Conoley & Garber, 1985;
Dowd et al., 2014; McWhirter & Horan, 1996;
Seepersad, 2005; Zhang, Fan, et al., 2018).
University students are in a transitional phase; they
often live away from home for the ﬁrst time in their lives,
and have various academic, ﬁnancial and social pres-
sures and concerns, which sets them apart from many
adults (Horgan, McCarthy, & Sweeney, 2013) and other
young people (Lim et al., 2020). Whilst students may
beneﬁt from interventions identi ﬁed in the earlier
reviews, it is unclear, which are most suitable and effec-
tive for students in a university setting. Most previous
reviews have contained interventions explicitly targeting
loneliness even though there may be broader, ‘indirect’
approaches to well-being that do not speci ﬁcally aim to
address loneliness but have important impacts on loneli-
ness (Mann et al., 2017). Due to the scarcity of studies
targeting students, in this narrative systematic review
our aim was to identify, describe and analyse the effec-
tiveness of intervention studies addressing loneliness in
university students (either directly or indirectly). The
research questions were: (a) What are the characteristics
of interventions that address levels of loneliness among
students in higher education (university)? (b) What is
the evidence for the effectiveness of interventions that
alleviate loneliness among university students?
Methods
Protocol and registration
The protocol for this review was registered with the PROSPERO
database (CRD42020180867) (Available from: https://www.
crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/display_record.php?ID=CRD420201
80867). This review was conducted according to the Preferred
Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses
(PRISMA) statement; Moher, Liberati, Tetzlaff, Altman, &
Group, 2009).
Search strategy
Searches were conducted on four electronic databases from
inception: PsycINFO, Medline, Web of Science and Applied
Social Science Index and Abstracts (ASSIA) on 22nd April 2020.

Reference lists of full text articles were screened for additional
relevant studies. Duplicates and nonEnglish titles were
removed using a reference management software to provide an
initial list of articles. Titles and abstracts were screened inde-
pendently by two reviewers (OE and CD). Discrepancies were
discussed between reviewers, and a third opinion (HT) was
sought in the event a discrepancy could not be resolved.
Eligibility criteria and study selection
Journal articles and doctoral dissertations were included only if
they met the inclusion criteria speci ﬁed in Table S2. Studies
implementing an intervention with loneliness as an outcome
and investigating undergraduate or postgraduate students at a
higher education institute were included. ‘Higher education ’
refers to stages 6, 7 and 8 of the International Standard Classi ﬁ-
cation of Education structure, and includes bachelor ’s or equiv-
alent, master or equivalent and doctorate or equivalent. This
excludes all primary and secondary education. We did not limit
our results by age of participant as to capture an accurate rep-
resentation of the student population.
Following the review of titles and abstracts, articles were
assessed by reviewing the full text. The inter-rater reliability
(IRR) at title an abstract screen was 78.14% (143/183). All 183
articles were included in the full text screen to ensure a thor-
ough process. At full text screen, IRR was 94.5% (173/183). Ten
articles with discrepancies were discussed among the research
team.
Quality assessment
Risk of bias was assessed at both study level and at out-
come level. The revised Cochrane risk of bias tool for ran-
domised trials (ROB2; Sterne et al., 2019) was used to
assess randomised experimental studies. The Risk of Bias In
Nonrandomised Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I; Sterne
et al., 2016) was used to assess nonrandomised experimen-
tal studies. Studies deemed to have ‘critical’ risk of bias were
excluded from data synthesis. Two reviewers (CD and OE)
assessed all studies independently then collaborated to iden-
tify discrepancies. If disagreements remained, a third opinion
was sought (HT).
Data extraction and synthesis
Data extracted from each study included: author, journal, year
of publication, location, study type, subgroup, intervention,
control, measurement tool, number of participants consented
(% male), mean age of participants, % drop-outs preinterven-
tion, group sizes at pretest (% male), groups sizes at post-test (%
male), group sizes at follow up (% male), % drop out from pretest
to follow up, mean age (range) at follow up, measurement
points, baseline and postintervention (follow-up) outcomes val-
ues, effect sizes (calculated difference in mean scores), p-values
and evidence of a difference. A pilot extraction was performed on
a small sample.
Attempts were made to contact ﬁve authors in the event of
missing data, e.g., on planned analysis, but these were unsuc-
cessful. All data was extracted by two reviewers (OE and CD)
independently, then results were compared for discrepancies. A
third opinion (HT) was sought in the event of a disagreement.
Given the use of two reviewers, blinding was not performed. An
Excel spreadsheet was used to extract data; no other data man-
agement tools were used.
A meta-analysis was not possible due to the heterogeneity of
studies. Instead, a narrative synthesis of ﬁndings was per-
formed to investigate the heterogeneity across the primary stud-
ies. Our reporting takes into consideration the Synthesis
Without Meta-analysis (SwiM) reporting guidelines (Campbell
et al., 2020).
Results
Study selection
Following removal of nonEnglish language and duplicate
articles, 6917 articles remained for screening by title
and abstract (See Flow diagram in Figure S1). 182 arti-
cles underwent full-text screening during which 155
were excluded, leaving 27 articles. Four additional arti-
cles were included after searching reference lists of full-
text articles. Twenty-eight studies measuring loneliness
quantitatively and three studies assessing loneliness
qualitatively were included in the quality assessment.
Findings from studies using quantitative
measurement of loneliness
Study quality. Mattanah, Brooks, Brand, Quimby, and
Ayers ( 2012) was found to be a secondary analysis of a
study already included in our review (Mattanah
et al., 2010), so was not assessed. Quality assessment of
studies using quantitative data collection is presented in
Figure S2 (randomised control trials, n = 15) and Fig-
ure S3 (nonrandomised designs, n = 12). The studies by
Mohamed and Ghany ( 2012), Brodkin, Shrier, Alger,
Layman, and Buxton (1983) and Yildiz and Duyan (2022)
were found to have an overall critical risk of bias arising
mostly from missing data, or from poor quality reporting
and small sample sizes; these articles were removed
from further review.
Of the remaining 25 studies, seven were judged as
having overall high or serious risk of bias and 10 were
judged as having overall moderate risk or some con-
cerns. Of the studies judged to have a high or serious
risk of bias, most were due to a lack of blinding of partici-
pants. As all measurements of loneliness were self-
reported, knowledge of the intervention may have in ﬂu-
enced participants ’ responses. Therefore, ﬁndings of
studies judged as having high or serious risk of bias were
interpreted cautiously. All studies other than Mascaro
et al. ( 2018), in which all analyses were conducted using
blinding, were judged as either moderate risk or some
concerns for risk of reporting bias. No further articles
were removed due to concerns of risk of bias.
Study characteristics. Of 25 studies using quantitative
measurements of loneliness, 16 were randomised con-
trol trials (RCT), three were controlled pretest, post-test
designs (CPP) and six were pretest, post-test (PP) designs
without a control group. Seventeen of the 26 articles
were published within the past 10 years, the remainder
between 1985 and 2011.

Outcome measures. Tools used to measure reductions
in feelings of loneliness were relatively consistent across
the research, with most ( n = 12) using the Revised UCLA
Loneliness Scale or UCLA3. The UCLA3 consists of 20
items assessing subjective feelings of loneliness and feel-
ings of social isolation, using questions such as “How
often do you feel close to people? ”. Participants rate each
item on a scale of 1 (Never) to 4 (Often) (Russell, 1996).
Shorter/longer versions of UCLA were used in some
studies (Barber, 2011; Bruehlman-Senecal et al., 2020;
Dowd et al., 2014; Kocak, 2008; Lu et al., 2020; Woro-
betz et al., 2020; Xiao et al., 2021). Other tools used to
measure feelings of loneliness included one item of the
PERMA pro ﬁler (Smith et al., 2020), a three-item loneli-
ness Likert scale (Besse, 2016), The University of the
Philippines Loneliness Assessment Scale (UPLAS) (Ste-
wart, Dispenza, Parker, Chang, & Cunnien, 2014), the
Chinese College Student Loneliness Scale (CCSLS;
Zhang, Fan, et al., 2018) and the short version of the
Social and Emotional Loneliness Scale for Adults
(SELSA-S; Youseﬁpour & Samarin, 2021). Measurement
points varied. A pre- and post-test measurements only
were taken in 11 studies. An additional midtest mea-
surement was implemented in seven studies (including
Barber ( 2011) in which multiple midtest measurements
were taken). A further follow-up measure was taken in
eight studies. The length of time between post-test mea-
surements and follow-up measurements varied from
2 weeks (Conoley & Garber, 1985; Dowd et al., 2014)t o
around 6 months (Gold et al., 2019; Mattanah
et al., 2010; Seepersad, 2005).
Intervention characteristics. Eighteen studies explored
the effectiveness of a single intervention whereas seven
studies compared the effectiveness of multiple interven-
tions. In total, 25 studies covered 34 interventions (inter-
ventions have been labelled numerically in Table S4).
While the aim of eight interventions was solely to reduce
levels of loneliness (5, 6, 7, 16, 22, 23, 24, 25), most used
loneliness as one measure of intervention effectiveness
among others, such as happiness (17, 28), anxiety (2,
17, 34), adjustment to college (4), sense of belonging
(18), self-esteem (8, 9, 19, 20, 31, 32), con ﬁdence (26),
overall wellness (1, 27, 30, 33), empathy (30), depression
(2, 8, 9, 10, 11, 31), compassion (29), responsibility (21)
and smartphone use (12, 13, 14,15).
The nature of the interventions followed common
themes: some provided mainly psychoeducation (5, 6, 7,
8, 9, 10, 11, 16, 18, 24, 25, 28) some implemented social
support groups in which students could discuss prob-
lems or experiences (4, 26, 32, 30), some provided an
opportunity for social interaction with other students
outside of a support group, with or without a speci ﬁc
activity or topic of discussion (2, 12, 17, 19, 31, 33, 34)
and others implemented re ﬂective exercises, such as
mindfulness (1, 3, 20, 22, 23, 27) or mindful movements
(e.g. yoga or Mind –Body-Qigong exercises) (13, 14, 29).
Where an intervention comprised a mix of two or more
elements, such as psychoeducation and social support
(26), we have classed them according to the main or dis-
tinguishing element.
The majority ( n = 28) of interventions were imple-
mented in a group setting, whereas a smaller number
(n = 6) were implemented on an individual. Two group
interventions were delivered online or digitally (19, 31)
as were two individual interventions (16, 20). Of the
group interventions, group sizes varied from two partici-
pants (31) to 112 participants (28).
Time commitments required from students to partake
in each intervention varied. One intervention was a three-
day retreat (29). Otherwise, the longest duration of any
intervention session was 180 min (17), with others lasting
between 30 and 120 min. Overall, support group inter-
ventions appeared to require longer sessions whereas
individual interventions were found to use shorter ses-
sions. One intervention consisted of one session of psy-
choeducation to the individual, with an expectation that
they engage in physical activity over the following 2 weeks.
No time commitment was speciﬁed (18).
Of the studies that included a control condition
(n = 19), most ( n = 12) did not implement an intervention
for this group. Instead, measurements coincided with
those taken from the intervention group. Seven studies,
however, created an alternative intervention to act as a
control condition. These included the provision of gen-
eral information (Besse, 2016; Dowd et al., 2014; Mat-
tanah et al., 2010) an educational course not aimed at
reducing loneliness (Kocak, 2008; Smith et al., 2020)
and a self-help discussion (McWhirter & Horan, 1996).
Two studies offered the control group access to the inter-
vention after a period or completion by the intervention
group (Bruehlman-Senecal et al., 2020; Youse ﬁpour &
Samarin, 2021).
Study ﬁndings. Evidence from the RCTs suggests that
most interventions had an effect on loneliness outcomes,
but the magnitude of the bene ﬁt is unclear (see
Table S4). These included six group-delivered psychoed-
ucation interventions (5, 6, 8, 9, 15, 21), ﬁve re ﬂective
exercises (3, 13, 14, 20, 22), one support group interven-
tion (4) and four social interaction interventions (2, 12,
17, 19). Controlled pre –post (CPP) and pre –post (PP)
studies also suggest that group-delivered psychoeduca-
tion (41), support group (38) and social interaction (31,
33, 34) may be effective in reducing loneliness. Table S4

from RCTs (7/12).
Studies involving students with pre-existing mental
health problems or feelings of loneliness (diagnosed or
self-declared) used a range of interventions including
social interaction (2), re ﬂective exercise (22), social sup-
port (26) and psychoeducation interventions (5, 6, 7, 8,
9, 10, 11, 16, 18, 21). However, only six interventions
targeting these students signi ﬁcantly reduced loneliness
score (5, 6, 8, 9, 21, 22).
Although no study directly compared the effect of a
single intervention across subgroups, reductions in feel-
ings of loneliness appeared consistent across groups.
For example, reductions in loneliness were seen across
students of all educational disciplines, in those with aut-
ism spectrum disorder (32) and in students from varied
geographic locations. Similarly, reductions in feelings of
loneliness were reported in studies sampling mostly
female students (8, 9) and in studies sampling mostly
male students (32).
Findings from studies using qualitative
assessment of loneliness
Study quality. Quality assessment of studies assessing
loneliness via qualitative methods is presented in Fig-
ure S3 (n = 3). Two studies (Clark & Brooms, 2018; Goet-
zel, Shelov, & Croen, 1983) had an overall moderate risk
of bias. Horgan et al. ( 2013) was found to have a serious
risk of bias due to missing data. The primary objective
was to assess a decrease in depressive symptoms; how-
ever, only a small number of participants (7 out of 118)
provided forum posts at follow up, some of which related
to the reduction of feelings of loneliness. All studies
using qualitative methods were judged as having moder-
ate risk of bias due to confounding factors.
Study characteristics. All interventions explored
through qualitative methods ( n = 3) were delivered as
part of a group, with one (27) delivered in an online for-
mat (see Table S5). One study recruited medical stu-
dents (Goetzel et al., 1983), one recruited black, male
students (Clark & Brooms, 2018) and one recruited stu-
dents scoring highly on the Centre for Epidemiological
Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) (Horgan et al., 2013).
Two studies were conducted in the U.S. (Clark &
Brooms, 2018; Goetzel et al., 1983) and one in Ireland
(Horgan et al., 2013). Mean ages of participants ranged
from 20.17 (Clark & Brooms, 2018) years to 24.6 years
(Goetzel et al., 1983).
Clark and Brooms ( 2018) collected data through
semistructured, open-ended interviews as opposed to
questionnaires or forums (Goetzel et al., 1983; Horgan
et al., 2013). Horgan et al. ( 2013) primarily used quanti-
tative methods to measure outcomes other than loneli-
ness, for example, reductions in depressive symptoms.
Reductions in loneliness were commented on qualita-
tively.
Data were collected at different points across the
research. Horgan et al. ( 2013) adopted pretest and post-
test measurements, whereas Clark and Brooms ( 2018)
and Goetzel et al. ( 1983) collected data post-test only.
Although this design prevents a comparison to baseline
levels of loneliness, participants were asked to give their
opinions of the intervention, not their levels of loneli-
ness.
Intervention characteristics. The types of interventions
implemented included support groups and increasing
social interaction (Table S5). Support groups included
the ‘Human Dimension Group ’ which adopted a face-to-
face discussion around student dif ﬁculties with support
from a facilitator (37). An online forum allowed students
to support each other virtually with access to online
resources (36). The ‘Black Male Scholars Programme ’
improved social interaction by encouraging students to
play sports together and by enlisting speakers on a num-
ber of topics (35).

interventions using qualitative data collection, with key
quotes from participants included in the publications.
The results of a study sampling students of black African
or Caribbean descent showed that bringing students
together who have a shared cultural experience can
reduce the feelings of loneliness (35). Feedback for the
Black Males Scholar Programme was generally positive
and focussed around reducing alienation of ethnic
minorities in universities.

improved feelings of loneliness: “It’s about empathy and

Discussion
This systematic review has investigated the characteris-
tics and effectiveness of interventions addressing loneli-
ness in university students. Loneliness is an indicator of
wellbeing and may lead to the development of mental
disorders if left untended (Mushtaq, Shoib, Shah, &
Mushtaq, 2014). Thirty-four interventions addressing
loneliness among university students were included, as
part of 25 studies. Only four studies, measuring a total
of eight interventions, targeted loneliness as a primary
outcome (Besse, 2016; Bruehlman-Senecal et al., 2020;
McWhirter & Horan, 1996; Zhang, Fan, et al., 2018).
Others had a broader wellbeing remit, some including a
number of components. Evidence from the RCTs sug-
gests that most interventions had a bene ﬁcial effect on
loneliness outcomes, including ones in the psychoedu-
cation, re ﬂective exercise, social interaction or social
support group categories. Two thirds of interventions
conducted in a group setting and measured quantita-
tively reduced loneliness scores. The measure of loneli-
ness used did not appear to affect the success of the
intervention, a ﬁnding reported also by Eccles and Qual-
ter (2021). Studies collecting data on loneliness via qual-
itative assessment highlighted that the sharing of
experiences with students of a similar background
reduced feelings of loneliness. However, none of the
studies followed up participants longitudinally. It is,
therefore, unclear whether the interventions had lasting
impact. As blinding to the intervention was not possible,
all included studies scored with at least ‘some concerns’
or ‘moderate risk of bias’ during quality assessment.
The current review represents an unexplored avenue
in loneliness research: 16 of 25 articles focusing on uni-
versity students were published within the past
10 years, the remainder being conducted earlier, over a
period spanning 27 years. The majority of the research
has been carried out in the U.S., including three of the
studies with a narrow focus on loneliness, despite
increased numbers of students all over the world suffer-
ing from loneliness, especially owing to COVID-19 pan-
demic.
The four major intervention strategies identi ﬁed (psy-
choeducation, increased social support, re ﬂective exer-
cises and increased social interaction) closely mirror
those identi ﬁed by Masi et al. ( 2011) and Osborn
et al. ( 2021), with ‘psychoeducation’ being broader but
encompassing the ‘addressing maladaptive social cogni-
tion’ strategy. Williams et al. ( 2021) grouped loneliness
into six categories based on a framework by Gardiner,
Geldenhuys, and Gott ( 2018), with social facilitation and
animal therapy categorised separately. In the current
review, these were both considered to increase social
interaction.
Masi et al. (2011) found addressing maladaptive social
cognition was the most effective technique. Williams
et al. ( 2021) found ‘psychological’ interventions to be
effective for students, such as mindfulness-based thera-
pies, akin to ‘reﬂective exercises ’ here. However, their
included interventions had to comply with COVID-19
social distancing measures. Most interventions included
in the review by Eccles and Qualter ( 2021) reduced lone-
liness among young people. In RCTs, interventions
focusing on learning a new hobby or skill yielded the lar-
gest effect, followed by interventions aimed at social
skills training. Notably, most young people included in
the review were part of at-risk clinical samples or at-risk
nonclinical samples (Eccles & Qualter, 2021). In the cur-
rent review, 80% of interventions evaluated quantita-
tively, utilising social support, re ﬂective exercises or
increasing social interaction, were effective. Psychoedu-
cation type interventions failed to show similar ef ﬁcacy;
only 50% of interventions were deemed to be effective.
The majority of the psychoeducation interventions in
our review not showing a positive effect (7/14) were
tested in RCTs.
In support of psychoeducational techniques, Jones,
Hobbs, and Hockenbury ( 1982) concluded that loneli-
ness results from a lack of teachable social skills. A
review of elements of loneliness interventions focusing
on behavioural and cognitive responses and/or coping
strategies identi ﬁed that social skills and communica-
tion training was the most frequently employed elements
used to address putative mechanisms, such as negative
interpersonal appraisals, anxiety and social skills de ﬁ-
cits (K€all et al., 2020). Cognitive behavioural therapy was
used by some studies either as the treatment format or
as a theoretical base for the intervention (K €all
et al., 2020). Hickin et al. ( 2021) reported psychological
interventions to be bene ﬁcial but made no comparison
to other types of interventions. Psychoeducation inter-
ventions identi ﬁed in this review included cognitive
restructuring exercises and developing (social) skills
with which to tackle loneliness, for example, through
roleplaying or gami ﬁcation. Four effective interventions
out of 10, targeted at students with elevated loneliness,
were based on psychoeducation. Other interventions
were not solely focused on loneliness but had a broader
wellbeing remit (Conoley & Garber, 1985; Orchard, 1986;
Smith et al., 2020). This may account for some of the
variance between the current ﬁndings and those
reported by Masi et al. ( 2011), whose review included
only interventions targeting loneliness. Our ﬁndings
suggest that psychoeducation alone, without an oppor-
tunity to increase social connections, may not be suf ﬁ-
cient for students living in new social surroundings. For
example, one of the smartphone app users (Bruehlman-
Senecal et al., 2020) would have liked the app to support
connections with others due to their dif ﬁculties with
face-to-face introductions.
Social support groups allowed students to compare
experiences, discuss worries, manage stressors of uni-
versity and seek advice and support. Noticeably, an
intervention focused on creating a support group to
build friendships in the ﬁrst year of university demon-
strated a reduction in loneliness in students with a mean

Interventions increasing social interaction encour-
aged students to spend more time with others outside of
a support group, for example through playing sports or
partaking in creative exercises. It appears that the act of
coming together with other students helps reduce loneli-
ness. Nyqvist, Victor, Forsman, and Cattan ( 2016)
showed young people who simply have more contact
with others experience reduced levels of loneliness. In
support, a sense of social connectedness has been iden-
tiﬁed as a protective mechanism against developing
mental disorders (Hawkley & Cacioppo, 2010). The pro-
motion of friendships between adolescents has been
shown to halve the risk of developing depression over 6
to 12 months (Hill, Grif ﬁths, & House, 2015).
Virtual platforms, such as online chat rooms or smart-
phone apps, allow social connections to develop remo-
tely. Our results showed that online interventions
reduced student loneliness (Barber, 2011; Shaw &
Gant, 2002), suggesting virtual platforms to be bene ﬁ-
cial. However, literature regarding the impact of technol-
ogy, particularly of social media, on loneliness is mixed
(Hunt, Marx, Lipson, & Young, 2018; Pittman, 2015;
Yang, 2016). While promoting social connectedness
face-to-face seems to reduce student loneliness, perhaps
only speci ﬁc forms of virtual platform, like those identi-
ﬁed here, have a positive impact. Nevertheless, virtual
interventions showed exciting potential and may require
less resources than face-to-face interventions.
Reﬂective exercise interventions, such as meditation,
journal writing or mindful movement, encouraged stu-
dents to re ﬂect on their own feelings without interfer-
ence, differentiating them from cognitive restructuring.
Negative thought processes associated with loneliness
can be addressed by techniques such as increasing
awareness of self and surroundings (Cacioppo, Grippo,
London, Goossens, & Cacioppo, 2015). Other docu-
mented bene ﬁts of mindfulness include reduction of
anxiety, depression and mood disorders (Howarth,
Smith, Perkins-Porras, & Ussher, 2019). Whilst many of
the re ﬂective exercise interventions were implemented
among groups of students, supporting social connected-
ness, re ﬂective exercise interventions can be performed
alone. Hence, they may be more useful when group
meetings are not possible.
In support of group-based interventions, these allevi-
ate the loss of meaningful social group memberships
that results from moving to university. As explained by
Haslam et al. ( 2022), group memberships provide people
with social identity, which refers to a person ’s sense of
self that derives from their membership in one or more
social groups. Social identi ﬁcation supports the align-
ment with group norms and the collaboration with
others, i.e., it underlies meaningful group-based social
connections. Social identi ﬁcation with one or more
groups boosts self-esteem and personal control and pro-
vides meaning and purpose. Social connections made in
groups (e.g., whether associated with mindful move-
ment, a more interactive pursuit or a support group)
thus provide young people a sense of shared meaning,
purpose, support and efﬁcacy (Haslam et al., 2022). Fur-
thermore, social connectedness can reduce depression
and anxiety symptoms for those with (emerging) mental
health problems, which further protects against loneli-
ness (Saeri, Cruwys, Barlow, Stronge, & Sibley, 2018).
The subjective sense of connectedness to a group has
also been posited as the ‘active ingredient’ in social rela-
tionships (Saeri et al., 2018), which may explain why
group-based interventions reduce loneliness for stu-
dents who may otherwise lack close friends at univer-
sity.
Strength and limitations
To our knowledge, this is the ﬁrst systematic review to
explore interventions addressing loneliness in university
students. Following the original submission of this sys-
tematic review, two systematic reviews of interventions
aimed at reducing loneliness in young people have been
published (Eccles & Qualter, 2021; Osborn et al., 2021).
However, neither draw special attention to university
students.
The scope of our search included younger university
students, from the age of 17, to graduate university stu-
dents. As there is some overlap in age with college stu-
dents (aged 16 –18 years in the UK), our ﬁndings may
also be applicable to those in postsecondary educational
settings.
Our search strategy incorporated a range of search
terms, multiple databases and was not limited by the
date of publication. Two independent reviewers com-
pleted both screening and quality assessment of all
research ensuring reliability. Whilst previous systematic
reviews have focused on interventions with loneliness as
the primary outcome, our review included studies with
loneliness as either primary or secondary outcome, thus
capturing all relevant research in this area and drawing
attention to interventions, which may serve multiple
purposes.
The reasons why some interventions were not effec-
tive may be because they were not developed speci ﬁ-
cally to reduce loneliness, and because the studies
were not powered to detect differences in secondary
outcomes. Grey literature searches were not performed
due to time constraints. Although some nonpublished
doctoral dissertations were identi ﬁed, the majority were
journal publications, retrieved from databases with
mostly peer-reviewed articles. Our review may, there-
fore, suffer from positive publication bias (Song
et al., 2009).
We were unable to perform a meta-analysis due to the
heterogeneity and quality of studies. A reduction in lone-
liness was somewhat comparable between studies using
identical measurement tools (e.g., 20 item UCLA3) but
was not possible between studies using different tools.
Further to this, the UCLA3, the UPLAS Loneliness Scale,
Chinese college student loneliness scale and 3 item Lik-
ert scale for loneliness were found to have coef ﬁcient
alphas for reliability of .89 to .94 (Russell, 1996), .93
(Tharayil, 2012), .86 (Zhang, Fan, et al., 2018) and .89
(Besse, 2016), respectively, making any objective com-
parison difﬁcult.
Interventions were classi ﬁed under broad groupings,
between which will be some overlap as most appeared to
include components of multiple categories.

Implications for future research
The majority of research identi ﬁed was deemed at an
increased risk of bias due to quasi-experimental study
designs. Further studies adopting a randomised con-
trolled trial design are needed, especially among stu-
dents with elevated loneliness or mental health
problems. Further research is also needed to compare
the effect of a given intervention across multiple sub-
groups, and to evaluate the effectiveness of an interven-
tion longitudinally. Due to the lack of research in BAME
students and those with autism or disability, further
experimental studies should be conducted in these sub-
groups. Evidence demonstrates these groups are partic-
ularly vulnerable to feelings of loneliness (Kennedy &
Barker, 2020; Reed, Giles, Gavin, Carter, & Osborne,
2016). Furthermore, research should take the complex-
ity of loneliness into consideration (Lim et al., 2020),
explore in-depth the mechanisms of successful interven-
tions and identify barriers that prevent at risk students
from participating in interventions.
Research is shifting focus from traditional methods of
targeting loneliness, to the inclusion of digital interven-
tions (Shah, Nogueras, Woerden, & Kiparoglou, 2019).
Future research might combine the use of technology
with other intervention types, such as facilitating sup-
port groups via mobile applications. Virtual communica-
tion may remove potential barriers associated with the
uptake of interventions, such as physical distance,
time/space/facilitator availability and student con ﬁ-
dence in meeting strangers.
Lastly, one of the intervention studies (McWhirter &
Horan, 1996) differentiated between social loneliness, a
lack of a supportive network, and intimate or emotional
loneliness, a lack of an intimate relationship, in line
with a growing body of research (Hyland et al., 2019;
van Tilburg, 2021). Although tailoring interventions to
subtypes of loneliness was not effective in this student
sample, future studies should take into account the dif-
ferent dimensions of loneliness (Cacioppo et al., 2015).
For this purpose, using a loneliness measure that differ-
entiates between social and emotional loneliness, such
as de Jong Gierveld loneliness scale, would be bene ﬁcial
(Penning, Liu, & Chou, 2014). The UCLA measure
focuses only on the social dimension.
Conclusions – Implications for universities
Loneliness has detrimental effects to mental and cogni-
tive health and is prevalent in the student population.
The adolescent brain continues to mature throughout
adolescence up to age of 25 (Arain et al., 2013). Providing
support to young students struggling with loneliness
may hinder the development of other risky behaviours,
e.g. alcohol or drug abuse (Stickley, Koyanagi, Koposov,
Schwab-Stone, & Ruchkin, 2014) or internet addiction
(Zhang et al., 2018).
COVID-19 related restrictions have worsened the situ-
ation for students. Although psychoeducation may be
less effective for the general student population than for
at risk or clinical populations in other settings and/or
age groups, interventions that boost social skills and
address maladaptive social cognitions are more relevant
than ever. Young people entering university now and
over the next few years have had fewer chances of inter-
acting with peers and building quality relationships in
shared physical spaces. Educational institutions cater-
ing for 16 –18-year-olds would be well placed to support
this process.
University campuses are ideally placed to tackle lone-
liness among students as a preventative or targeted
strategy and a solution to stem the sharp rise in the
demand for mental health/support services. Preventing
or reducing loneliness may also have a positive impact
on performance ﬁgures, such as educational attain-
ment, completion rates and employment prospects (Mat-
thews et al., 2019, 2021).
The current review shows that universities can choose
from a variety of strategies. Most successful interventions
among students foster social connectedness and are con-
ducted in a group setting. Importantly, interventions
need not only focus on, or be advertised as focusing on,
loneliness but can have a broader wellbeing remit. Mea-
sures that reduce academic stress are likely to support
the joining and identifying with social groups (McIntyre,
Worsley, Corcoran, Harrison Woods, & Bentall,2018).
Given the strong social pressure for young people to
appear connected (Achterbergh et al., 2020), admitting
to loneliness may be difﬁcult (Kerr & Stanley, 2021). Fear
of stigmatisation could deter uptake of public interven-
tions. Virtual platforms are, therefore, a promising med-
ium for delivering interventions privately and
anonymously or where social gatherings are restricted
(Lim et al., 2020).
Further intervention studies should be conducted
with loneliness as the primary endpoint in a broader
range of countries and cultures, covering students with
elevated loneliness, of different ethnic backgrounds and
those with autism or a disability.
