Abstract
The case study concerns Singapore’s Chinatown which occupies an important place in the country’s history and has acquired major
signi"cance as a tourist attraction. Whilst much of the area was saved from the urban redevelopment characteristic of Singapore in the
period since its independence and its conservation has become an o$cial priority, approaches adopted towards the presentation and
interpretation of the site have changed over time and are now a matter of public debate. Interest has increased recently as a result of
the announcement of a new development programme which is outlined after a brief history of Chinatown. An account of the responses
and concerns about the future then follows. Despite the very distinctive characteristics of Chinatown, events there raise general
questions about the use of heritage as a tourist attraction including matters of authenticity and local involvement as well as those
relating to the applicability of the life cycle model; these themes are addressed in the "nal section which presents some general
conclusions based on the particular circumstances of the case. ( 2000 Elsevier Science Ltd. All rights reserved.

1. Introduction
The Chinese diaspora has resulted in a large popula-
tion living outside of China, principally in Asia with
signi"cant numbers in Europe and North America. It has
been estimated that this population numbered over 30
million in 1990 (Pan, 1998) and continued expansion is
likely to have taken place since then. Early Chinese settlers
overseas often gathered together in communities, many of
which still exist as Chinatowns throughout the world and
represent important tourist sites in cities including Lon-
don, San Francisco, Sydney, Vancouver and Yokohama.
The characteristics of such ethnic enclaves as living com-
munities and tourist attractions has recently attracted the
interest of academics like Conforti (1996) whose studies
address questions of place presentation, promotion,
planning and participation.
Many of these issues are raised by the case of Sin-
gapore’s Chinatown which has played an important part
in the country’s history, with an o$cial commitment to
its conservation since the 1980s. It continues to evolve as
a living and working community, but that community is
under threat from tourism as the government intensi"es
e!orts to develop and promote it as a destination for
tourists with the launch of an ambitious programme for
its revitalisation which critics have argued will turn the
area into a theme park.
This study considers some of the important features of
Chinatown’s history and changes in attitudes towards its
conservation. The "nal section returns to the broader
themes of participatory planning, authenticity and desti-
nation life cycles in an attempt to extend the discussion
and permit some more general conclusions to be derived.
While acknowledging the uniqueness of Singapore’sC h i n a -
town, these would appear to be matters that a!ect many
such areas of this type and have relevance for the continuing
debate about urban heritage conservation in general and
the use of that which is conserved as a tourist attraction.

Singapore’s total, respectively, with contemporary maps

2.2. Li ving conditions
As the population expanded, there were mounting
concerns about living conditions in Chinatown with
evidence of overcrowding, congestion and pollution.
Many inhabitants were coolies or labourers who shared
the same sleeping space in cubicles in coolie lodging
houses with little sanitation and limited access to fresh
water. They faced poverty, malnutrition and had no
proper medical facilities, despite the high incidence of
contagious diseases including tuberculosis. In addition,
secret societies, gang crime, opium smoking and prostitu-
tion were rampant. The area became the centre of the
Chinese coolie trade where unsuspecting coolies were
bought and sold, then shipped on overseas. The streets
were also full of hawkers selling a variety of goods from
cooked food to household wares, operating on foot all
day and later crowding narrow passages with their stalls.
Yet early travellers were drawn to Chinatown and
found street life and the roadside culture colourful and
entertaining, while still being shocked by the squalor
present in many parts. It seemed to epitomise the exotic
Orient and &the diversity of forms, shapes, colours and
people of the street scenes best reinforced the Westerner’s
romantic conception of the picturesque ’ (Savage, 1992,
p. 17).
Conditions deteriorated further after the Second
World War and Japanese Occupation when parts of the
town were badly bombed, destroying homes and busi-
nesses. There was a government enquiry in 1947 (Kiat,
1947) and a later report by Kaye (1960) documented the
appalling life for many inhabitants of Chinatown. The
particular concern was about overcrowding which was
partly a result of the subtenanting practice of dividing
and further subdividing rooms, the most dilapidated
buildings often the worst a !ected. The back-to-back
rows upon rows of shophouses had steep staircases lead-
ing to the living quarters above which were divided by
partitions into cubicles, about the width of two double
beds. Cubicles were often shared by households with as
many as seven family members. There was a common
kitchen, bathroom and lavatory in each building and
originally night soil had to be carried through the house
when collected because there were no back lanes. It
is perhaps worth bearing these facts in mind in any
discussion of the restoration of Chinatown and the
invitation of promotional literature to come and see
Chinatown as it once was.
2.3. Change and impro vement
Some improvement was attempted by the colonial
authorities, but signi "cant change only came with
Singapore’s independence "rstly from the British and
then from the Malayan Federation in 1965. The govern-
ment was committed to raising the living standards of the
population and the public provision of better housing
became a key objective. A number of development agen-
cies and United Nations advisers were also proposing
comprehensive urban renewal programmes which de-
manded extensive land clearance, especially in the city
centre. The Urban Renewal Department of the Housing
and Development Board, the forerunner of the present
Urban Redevelopment Authority (URA), was given re-
sponsibility for Chinatown and started a programme of
redevelopment and resettlement in association with other

said in 1980 that it was not practical to preserve most of
the buildings in Chinatown because of their poor struc-
tural condition (Powell, 1994). Conservation was con-
sidered incompatible with development and prevented an
area from reaching its full economic potential.
3. Conservation in the 1980s
3.1. A new attitude
There was a change in attitude in the 1980s as o$cials
recognised the value of conservation and popular aware-
ness of the possible disappearance of irreplaceable as-
pects of the country’s heritage increased, an indication of
the latter being the founding of the Singapore Heritage
Society in 1998. Chinatown was acknowledged as
a unique district, worthy of preservation in the interests
of the resident population, but also as a tourist site. The
year of 1984 was seen as a turning point when the
government accepted a report by the URA setting
up a pilot conservation project in the Tanjong Pagar
district, one especially rich in shophouse architecture.
Smith (1988) has explained the shift in government
policy towards conservation as a result of a sudden
decline in visitor arrivals and its impact on hotel occu-
pancy levels. Inbound tourism had been increasing at an
average rate in excess of 10 per cent up until 1982 when
there was a fall of 4.5 per cent, leading to the danger of
overcapacity in the accommodation sector as hotels were
doubling their room stock in anticipation of continued
growth. In response to this potential crisis, the govern-
ment appointed a Tourism Task Force to consider the
problems and possible solutions. The Report of the
Tourism Task Force in 1984 (Wong et al., 1984) high-
lighted the three main problems of perceptions of
Singapore as an expensive destination with few tourist
attractions and adversely a!ected by foreign protection-
ism. It recognised that some historical sites had
disappeared because of development and there was
a subsequent risk of Singapore losing its distinctive heri-
tage which appealed to many visitors. In the words of the
report, &in our e!ort to build up a modern metropolis, we
have removed aspects of our Oriental mystique and
charm which are best symbolised in old buildings, tradi-
tional activities and bustling road side activities ’ (p.6).
There was thus a strong economic case for preserving
and restoring Chinatown and other historical sites, one
of the Report’s principal recommendations.
3.2. Conser vation initiatives
Conservation became a key theme of subsequent stud-
ies such as those commissioned by the Singapore Tourist
Promotion Board (STPB) from Pannell, Kerr, Forster
Consultants (1986) and another prepared by the Paci"c
Area Travel Association (PATA, 1985) which together
with the Task Force report formed the basis for the
o$cial Tourism Product Development Plan prepared in
1986 (MTI and STPB, 1986). The Plan was a joint initiat-
ive of the STPB and the Ministry of Trade and Industry
and proposed using six themes to promote and develop
Singapore as a tourist destination with the intention of
attracting more visitors and spending. The Exotic East
and Colonial Heritage were themes which involved the
conservation of various historic areas and places includ-
ing Chinatown and incorporated proposals for its re-
vitalisation.
A Conservation Plan was devised for Chinatown
which had the following objectives:
(a) To retain and restore buildings of historical and
architectural signi"cance.
(b) To improve the general physical environment and
to introduce appropriate new features to further enhance
the identity of the area.
(c) To retain and enhance ethnic-based activities while
consolidating the area with new and compatible activities.

By the end of the 1980s then, Chinatown was assuming

continued promotion as a tourist attraction and more

4. Chinatown in the 1990s
4.1. Tourism 21
The Tourism Product Development Plan was to run

cance &can be enhanced if its various streets, buildings

4.4. Singapore New Asia branding
One aim guiding the authorities in their e !orts to

The e!ectiveness of such a branding exercise might be

Matters of design also arise and the need for harmoni-

ploying appropriate colours and materials. The restored

bright paints used externally which create an impression
of newness and of a stage set, contrasting markedly with
the dark and sombre hues of the older properties and
their worn appearance. Modern signs and other items
like air-conditioning units can also add a discordant
element.

In response to the expression of these views, the STB
defended its policies and sought to reassure those con-
cerned that the guiding philosophy was to&acknowledge,
respect and preserve the unique character of the area ’
with the proposed developments introducing &enhance-
ment elements that are enduring, genuine and sustain-
able, rather than gimmicky and short-lived ’. There had
been discussions with many interested parties and stake-
holders including the local Revitalisation of Chinatown,
Citizens Consultative and Chinatown Retailers Pro-tem
Committees as well as numerous o$cial agencies. A pro-
cess of consultation was said to be underway and feed-
back was invited (Anon, 1998).
At this stage, it is di$cult to comment on the extent to
which the most recent proposals will be implemented and
their consequences. Public interest has led to the an-
nouncement of further consultation with local residents
and other groups and there is a possibility of some
modi"cations being made. Nevertheless, it would appear
that Chinatown is changing and assuming a di !erent
identity. While places evolve over time and Chinatown’s
history has been one of constant and rapid change, the
concern remains that these changes and new identity are
being forced upon it by external agencies to create
a manufactured sense of place. The image of a theme park
is often invoked by critics with the Chinatown of the
future described as such an attraction in which locals
have no place, except as paid employees. Chinatown as
a community of residents and workers will thus cease to
exist, and there is a strong feeling that the people who
make up this community have not as yet been su$ciently
consulted about their views.
5. Conclusions
A study of Chinatown and its development raises inter-
esting and topical issues relating to the search for authen-
ticity in the conservation of heritage and its presentation
as a tourist attraction, the role of local people in the
planning process and the applicability of the life cycle
model. It illustrates the con#icting interests at work, the
need to achieve some kind of balance amongst them and
the di$culties of securing this. The history of Singapore,
its unique society of mixed races and particular political
culture all add to the complexity of the case and intro-
duce further tensions and layers of meaning. Whilst re-
cognising that each Chinatown and similar historic sites
will have a distinctive history, set of current concerns and
future development patterns, it is possible to draw some
general lessons from the case and these are discussed
below.
5.1. Local participation
Since Murphy (1985) stressed the importance of
greater involvement of the community in tourism devel-
opment, this has become a widely accepted aim amongst
planners with destination residents &being seen increas-
ingly as the nucleus of the tourism product ’ (Simmons,
1994, p. 98). Pearce, Moscardo and Ross (1996) write of
the need for a&more sophisticated understanding of how
communities react to the burgeoning phenomenon of
tourism’ (p. 1) in the pursuit of improved long-term
planning and discuss the value of a social representations
approach. The debate has since been extended to incor-
porate stakeholder theories and arguments about the
need to ensure that all local stakeholders contribute to
planning policy making in the interests of sustainability
(Sautter & Leisen, 1999).
While accepting the bene "ts and desirability of this
participation, its practicality and viability has also been
questioned and Taylor (1995) describes the perverseness
that &in approaching community members to become
participants in both the tourism product and its plann-
ing, residents are encouraged to become part of the
problem and then given the opportunity to become part
of the solution ’ (p. 488). There are also di $culties of
establishing appropriate and workable mechanisms for
consultation and involvement and evaluating its e !ec-
tiveness, especially in the developing world and Timothy
(1999) lists cultural and political traditions, poor eco-
nomic conditions, lack of expertise and lack of under-
standing by residents as constraints in the case of the
Indonesian destination of Yogyakarta. Many states may
also choose to set limits to the extent of public debate and
exercise control over the outcome, either openly or more
covertly. However, this problem is also evident in
the countries of the developed world as Bramwell and
Sharman (1999) reveal in their account of stakeholder
collaboration in a UK case study where&unequal power
relations remained among the stakeholders with the dis-
tribution of power weighted towards the authorities
rather than the residents’ (p. 412).
Timothy also presents a model of participatory
tourism planning which includes public consultation and
locally de"ned goals, input from other stakeholders, in-
volvement of locals in the bene "ts of tourism and the
education of residents. While there is some evidence of
such community participation in the decision making
process in Singapore, it appears to have been arranged at
a late stage in response to popular concern and adverse
publicity. Greater e !orts are now being made to
consult, involve and educate locals and the recent STB

tions in Chinatown thus suggest the existence of a gap
between planning theory and practice, as well as the
necessity of local involvement if tourism development is
to succeed in the long term.
Ideally, it is not only the inhabitants of Chinatown
who should have a say, but also the population of Sin-
gapore as a whole who represent important stakeholders
given the national signi "cance of the site. Teo writing
independently (1994) and with Huang (1995) has de-
scribed the absence of local participation in other
tourism projects in Singapore and the subsequent alien-
ation and exclusion of the resident population from what
become principally tourism venues. Whenever, and wher-
ever this happens, authenticity and meaning are lost,
possibly forever.
5.2. Authenticity and recreating the past
The case also illustrates some of the di $culties asso-
ciated with the concept of authenticity in the tourist
experience, suggested by MacCannell (1976) and sub-
sequently explored in a series of studies (Cohen, 1979;
Pearce & Moscardo, 1986; Selwyn, 1996). With the
emergence of an extensive literature on the topic, the
subject has acquired a complexity and ambiguity so that
satisfactory de"nitions appear elusive* especially when
postmodernist discussions are considered (Eco, 1986).
A recent article by Wang (1999) provides an excellent
summary of the alternative ways of assessing authenticity
and o!ers a three-fold typology. Objective authenticity
refers to that of originals, constructive authenticity is that
which is projected onto objects or experiences by the
tourists themselves or tourist industry and existential
authenticity is that which arises from tourist activity and
participation. However, it is not just the tourists and the
industry, but also the state which determines forms
of authenticity (Bruner, 1994) with local views often
overlooked in this process.
Commentators suggest that tourists claim to search for
authentic objects, sites and experiences, but often accept
as authentic what might be dismissed as fake or bogus by
experts and intellectuals; the authenticity they seek is
thus symbolic rather than real. Objective authenticity is
increasingly uncommon, being replaced by its construc-
tive and existential forms. In some ways, reality has
ceased to exist as we inhabit a postmodern world of
simulacra which is well suited to a tourism industry that
deals in images and idealised representations.
Tourist appreciation and understanding of authentic-
ity is a question that merits further study as does the
approach of attractions which claim to be o !ering it.
While accepting that those involved cannot be expected
to depict a site ’s past in all its squalor, they do have
certain responsibilities with regard to giving visitors an
accurate and meaningful sense of the history of a place
and its contemporary realities. It is important for public
and private sector providers to try to understand and
meet the needs of both residents and visitors where pos-
sible whilst also striving for honesty and integrity of
presentation and interpretation so that ambiguity about
the type of authenticity on o !er is reduced. There is
a danger that presentation becomes a selective process
which re#ects the interests of those making the selection
(Barthel, 1989) rather than the local population or indeed
the tourists themselves, and that authenticity is manipu-
lated accordingly.
Within the context of Chinatown, part of the past that
the authorities wish to see return and sometimes promise
to the tourists is a lively street life, usually described as
&vibrant’, but the original atmosphere obviously cannot
be recovered. The former hustle and bustle on the streets
was a result of the dense population and overcrowded
conditions which forced people out on to the footways
and roads for leisure and commerce with a bazaar econ-
omy of day and night markets. Such trading disappeared
as a result of the government ’s housing and urban re-
newal policies, and the establishment of a formal econ-
omy and planned city displaced the &itinerant hawkers,
pedlars and roving traders’ (Savage, 1992, p. 19). As well
as traders, traditional entertainments and activities like
glove puppet shows and story-telling have also died out,
or are in the process of doing so, as described by former
practitioners in a recent oral history project (Lo-Ang
& Chua, 1992).
While much of the physical landscape is capable of
being restored and has acquired a degree of constructed
authenticity, ways of living and street life cannot be so
easily represented and it is unlikely that many residents
or visitors would want to return to the harsh conditions
of Chinatown and other historic sites at the turn of the
century. The problem then is determining what and
whose authenticity is presented to the visitor. As the
authorities and tourism industry seek to create an atmo-
sphere and environment which evokes the past and
reintroduce traditional activities in modi "ed forms,
Chinatown might be seen as an attempt to devise and
present a form of selective and possibly distorted authen-
ticity which avoids areas of contention and controversy.
The emphasis is on making the place an appealing one
for visitors who are encouraged to spend their time and
money there.
There is the additional challenge of the fact that those
traditional traders who have survived are faced with high
rental costs of restored properties and increases of 100 to
200 per cent on each occasion. For example, the rent for
an 800 sq ft corner shop unit rose from $80 a month to
$5000 in 1998 for the retailer after the shophouse was
renovated (Li, 1998). These charges make it di$cult for
the small scale businesses to survive with their places
being taken by fast food outlets, expensive restaurants

ent may not always match that sought by the tourist who
may also be dissatis"ed with the contrived versions of the
past which lies uneasily alongside. Additional research
into the actual responses of visitors to Chinatown and
their experiences there is necessary, however, to test such
observations while the attitudes of residents and the
formulation of those attitudes also demands further
study.
5.3. Product life cycles
The life cycle model is commonly used to analyse the
evolution of tourism destinations and several versions
have been proposed since the 1960s (Alex Tooman, 1997),
although Butler’s (1980) remains perhaps the most in#u-
ential. He described the stages of the life cycle as being
those of exploration, involvement, development, consoli-
dation, stagnation, and decline or rejuvenation. The con-
cept is not without its critics, however, who claim that it
can only be a guide for planners and marketers (Cooper,
1994) while others argue that it has lost any value it once
possessed. Da Conceicao and Roque Aquas (1997) sum-
marise the limitations as &the absence of a prescriptive
model that can be widely used and unanimously accep-
ted; the di$culty in determining to which stage products
and services belong; the lack of data to support the
theory; and the concept’s subjective nature’ (p. 14).
It is not the purpose of this paper to evaluate the
product life cycle in detail, the subject already of some
debate in the academic press (Singh, 1998), but it can be
concluded that Singapore’s Chinatown does conform to
the model in many respects in that it appears to have
followed the pattern from early discovery and explora-
tion to maturity in its history as a tourist attraction. It is
also a concept employed by the tourism authorities who
frequently make use of terms such as revitalisation and
rejuvenation to describe their policies. Present develop-
ments in Singapore’s Chinatown can be seen as a recog-
nition by the tourism authorities that the site has arrived
at the stage of stagnation and possible decline and that
the proposed changes are necessary to begin a process of
rejuvenation.
However, whilst the life cycle model appears to be
a useful instrument to conceptualise a destination’se v o l u -
tion, the question of whether such a complex entity as
Chinatown and its history can be treated like a consumer
product raises some doubts about the appropriateness of
its application to places which are also communities. As
such, these sites undergo continual change and this con-
stant development may give rise to shifts in their identity,
both in substance and perception, as tourist destinations.
The life cycle model is perhaps too rigid and in#exible to
capture and explain these dynamics.
Chinatown thus represents the challenges facing both
conservation agencies and those responsible for present-
ing and promoting the heritage which is conserved to
visitors. It provides an illustration of the possibilities and
pitfalls of both processes and o !ers some interesting
insights into the debate about participatory planning and
the search for authenticity, as well as the appropriateness
of the life cycle model. The ability to meet these chal-
lenges e !ectively in Singapore remains debatable with
evidence of the bene "ts of conservation initiatives and
their limitations, as well as both successful and inappro-
priate tourism development and moves towards greater
community participation. Questions of whose heritage is
being preserved, in what manner and for what purpose
need to be addressed, however, to ensure that the China-
town which emerges in the new millenium will still be
a living and working community that attracts visitors for
itself. Chinatown and similar sites should not be allowed
to become stages for the telling of stories devised by the
tourism industry to maximise the sale of its products and
sanctioned by government in pursuit of wider objectives;
in this way, locals are denied their rightful opportunity to
participate and the visitor experience is devalued.
