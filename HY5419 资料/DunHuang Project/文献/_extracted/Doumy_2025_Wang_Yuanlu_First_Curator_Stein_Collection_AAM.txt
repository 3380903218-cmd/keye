Wang Yuanlu, the first ‘curator’ of the Stein Collection’s Dunhuang 
corpus 
Mélodie Doumy 
 
Abstract 
Wang Yuanlu 王圓籙, the individual who accidentally stumbled upon the ‘library cave’ or Cave 17 at the Mogao 
Caves near Dunhuang in June 1900, is behind one of the world’s most significant discoveries. Yet, in the years 
that followed, he was also responsible for the scattering of the cave’s contents, selling large chunks to Marc Aurel 
Stein in 1907 and to Paul Pelliot in 1908. How could the self-appointed guardian of this major Buddhist complex 
part with one of what is often described as its crowning jewels? This article  delves i nto Wang Yuanlu’s 
motivations and highlights his agency, demonstrating that he was instrumental in shaping the now so-called Stein 
collection. One of t he key sources for this investigation are  the published writings of Marc Aurel Stein , which 
provide the most detailed descriptions of Wang and his activities during the period of the dispersal of the contents 
of Cave 17. These are supplemented by information drawn from the relevant parts of Stein’s diaries, Paul Pelliot’s 
writings and primary sources and recent scholarship in Chinese that shed a different light on Wang’s doings. The 
article starts by studying Wang’s relationship to the Mogao Caves and their wider ecosystem. It then looks at the 
subsequent dispersal of the newly found hoard through  his transactions with Stein in 1907  and 1914, which are 
contrasted with his dealings with Pelliot.  
 
Introduction 
In June 1900, an individual named Wang Yuanlu 王圓籙 (1850?–1931) noticed a crack in the 
wall of the corridor leading to a large cave, Cave 16, at the Mogao Caves site, near Dunhuang, 
as he was conducting some restoration work. Upon checking what was behind the exposed 
plaster, he discovered a door behind w hich a small chamber had been excavated in the rock. 

2 
 
Now commonly referred to as Cave 17 or the ‘library cave’, this room is thought to have been 
sealed off at the beginning of the eleventh century. It turned out to be a real “time capsule of 
Silk Road history,”1 containing tens of thousands of manuscripts, as well as printed documents, 
paintings on paper and silk, textiles and other Buddhist artefacts.  
Wang rapidly alerted local officials to this discovery, but no immediate actions were 
taken. In 1904, orders were sent to keep the collection in place, and he remained de facto 
custodian of the cave. In 1910, an official from the Ministry of Education was dispatched to 
Dunhuang to transport all the manuscripts left in Cave 17 to Beijing for safekeeping. By then, 
the British-Hungarian archaeologist Aurel Stein (1862 –1943) and the French Sinologist Paul 
Pelliot (1878–1945) had already respectively visited the Mogao Caves in 1907 and 1908, and 
Wang had agreed to sell them both exceptionally large chunks of the repository’s contents. 
Later on, he parted with more items against payment. For this reason, Wang Yuanlu has been 
cast down in Chinese history as a villain, whose name, although associated with the discovery 
of Cave 17, is even more so linked to the dispersal of its contents. He has often been portrayed 
as being illiterate and unaware of the significance of the trove held in the cave, which resulted 
in him being duped by Western explorers. Others have simply considered him to be the prime 
example of a thief, who profited from the sale of Dunhuang relics. 2 In recent years, several 
Chinese scholars have started to show that Wang was driven by his piety and desire to protect 
the Mogao Caves. Yet he remains overall an obscure figure.

Both Marc Aurel Stein’s  personal narratives, Ruins of Desert Cathay  and On central 
Asian tracks, and the expedition reports that he wrote , Serindia and Innermost Asia, provide 
complementary accounts of his visit s to the Mogao Caves and his negotiations with Wang 
Yuanlu over the contents of Cave 17. These books play a key part  in shaping Stein’s self -
representation, as he glorifies his own actions and, in doing so, minimise s Wang’s role. 
However, when reading between the lines  and bringing in other contemporary sources , it 
becomes possible to see Wang Yuanlu having a much more important part in the care, 
arrangement and dispersal of th e cave’s contents. While Stein’s published  accounts had a 
‘performative’ dimension, they also contain information  about Wang’s custodianship of the 
caves, his relationship to the wider local community, his motivations for selling Cave 17’s 
contents, and the way he selected materials for the expl orer’s ‘examination’. Therefore, this 
article critically examines these writings, contrasting them (when relevant) with material from 
his travel diaries, as well as Pelliot’s account and other contemporaneous sources in Chinese.  
It highlights Wang Yuanlu’s agency , demonstrating that he was instrumental in shaping the 
now so-called Stein collection when it comes to the items removed from Mogao Cave 17. It 
thus proposes to build a fuller picture of Wang, the “priestly ‘librarian” 3  who could be 
considered as the first ‘curator’ of the cave’s contents.

The Mogao caves are located within close distance southeast of the oasis -town of 
Dunhuang 敦煌, which was established as a frontier garrison outpost in the second century 
BCE and came to be a strategic spot at the cross of the Northern and the Southern Silk Roads. 
The first cave is said to have been carved in 366 CE by a Buddhist pilgrim monk and, over the 
next millennium, almost one thousand caves and niches were gradually excavated in the

We have therefore seen that Wang, who was already acting as the self -appointed caretaker of 
the Mogao Caves , came to oversee the protection Cave 17 and its contents in a semi -official 
capacity in the years following his extraordinary chance discovery. Having attempted to alert 
several officials, he was instructed to provisionally look after the small cave and its contents, 
and locked it for safety. We will now demonstrate that he was not assuming this role as a lone 
Daoist monk but rather as a well -integrated member of the local religious community, 
including resident Buddhist monks.

Conclusion 
Wang Yuanlu, the man whose pious restoration work led to one of the most astonishing 
discoveries of the twentieth century, is a fascinating character. The present article attempts to 
provide a more nuanced understanding of him by examining his relationship to the Mogao 
Caves and their wider ecosystem, especially the community of local Buddhist monks and the 
lay donors that funded his enterprises, by looking at the way he drove the transactions with 
Stein during his first visit in 1907 and, finally by studyi ng the subsequent dispersal of Cave 
17’s contents and the acquisitions made by Stein when he returned to the site in 1914. We argue 
here that Wang is not so much an ‘obscure’ as an ‘obscured’ figure. 
When taking a closer look at Aurel Stein’s detailed writings as well as other sources, 
there are many hints of a much more complex personality. They certainly belie the simplistic 
image of a “quaint priest, with [a] curious mixture of pious zeal, naïve ign orance, and astute 
tenacity of purpose,”161 who was swayed to sell thousands of manuscripts, painting and other 
artefacts from Cave 17 because of the admiration he and Stein had in common for the monk 
Xuanzang or because he believed that he would “acquire spiritual merit by letting [Stein] take 
back to the old home of Buddhism some of the ancient manuscripts which chance had placed 
in his keeping.”162 Wang had much more agency than he usually is credited for, but he should 
not be reduced to a thief or even a shrewd b usinessman, who saw the content s of Cave 17 as 
mere commodities. According to  Justin Jacobs, he “sought to control access to his ‘goods,’ 
 
161 Stein, Ruins of Desert Cathay, 1912: p. 169. 
162 Stein, Ruins of Desert Cathay, 1912: p. 170. 

47 
 
carefully manage his ‘stock,’ and ‘sell’ them on his own terms.”163 This is not a completely fair 
characterisation of his actions, and most importantly, of the true motivation(s) behind them.  
Wang Yuanlu, who took upon himself to act as the guardian of the Mogao Caves when 
he arrived at the site and joined in the restoration efforts already initiated before his time, can 
also be considered the first ‘curator’ or ‘keeper’ of Cave 17 and its cont ents. He did alert the 
authorities of the existence of the newly found hoard and, having been left in charge of 
preserving the collection, protected it by installing a door with a lock and re-organised the room 
according to an arrangement that he deemed mo re suitable. Stein’s accounts misrepresent 
Wang as unaware of the nature of the cave’s contents, which is far from the truth. Wang Yuanlu 
collaborated closely with resident Buddhist monks, relying on their expertise to identify some 
materials, and lending them items for ritual purposes. He also handpicked fine specimens that

Cave 17, he would have had a good grasp of the cache’s contents. He is the only individual 
who ever had an overall picture of what it contained and how the materials were originally 
deposited. This insider knowledge came in handy in his negotiations with Stein. 
Equally important is the role Wang Yuanlu played in shaping the now so -called 
Dunhuang Stein collection. Throughout his transactions with Aurel Stein, Wang was concerned 
to control of the situation, setting up the conditions for the exchange. When Stein first visited 
the Thousand Buddha Caves in 1907, he was not allowed to freely search for materials in the 
repository. He had to rely on Wang’s choice of bundles, from which to make selections. At all 
times, Stein and Jiang Xiaowan had to act in quasi -secrecy, so as not to arouse suspicions. 
Wang Yuanlu was particularly reluctant when it came to the Buddhist canonical texts forming 
the bulk of the ‘regular bundles,’ which he had safely sandwiched between a bottom and top

Ultimately, Wang Yuanlu made a judgement call between the relative importance of 
the Mogao Caves site as a whole versus that of Cave 17’s contents. Enabling repair works to 
what had been a thriving Buddhist pilgrimage centre from the fourth to the fourteenth centuries 
was a work of piety to which he devoted his entire life, channelling all the money he collected 
through his begging tours and then the sale of Dunhuang manuscripts towards clearing off the 
sand obstructing the caves, repairing statues and paintings and restoring shrines around the site. 
Not only was his decision made from the perspective of living practice, as  opposed to that of 
scholarship, it was also made collectively. Although Wang obviously wanted to avoid drawing 
attention to his transaction with Stein, it seems likely that the Buddhist monks established at 
the site would have been in the know. In additio n, Wang Yuanlu consulted the donors 
supporting his initiatives, as he told Stein he was keen to do, facilitating more open transactions 
with Pelliot and other explorers afterwards. The implication here is that he acted with the accord 
and on behalf of the small religious community that he belonged to. It seems that Wang’s 
dealings with Stein, Pelliot and others did not diminish the esteem in which he was held by this
