Stein's Recording Angel — Miss F. M. G. Lorimer
HELEN WANG
In the Introductions to the Full Reports of his expeditions to Chinese Central Asia,1 Sir
Aurel Stein (1862—1943), professional as always, was careful to acknowledge the assistance of
all involved with the production of the reports. Most of his colleagues in the academic world
were men who to this day retain their positions as great scholars in the history of oriental
studies. The people who helped Stein in the remote sites of Xinjiang live on through his
affectionate, and often humorous, descriptions of them scattered throughout the Full
Reports and the popular abridged accounts of his expeditions. Yet very little is known of the
people who worked on a day-to-day basis with Stein, unpacking the many cases he sent to
the British Museum, sorting through the various groups of often unfamiliar material,
documenting the Stein collection, arranging exhibitions of objects and photographs relating
to Stein's expeditions, as well as carrying out personal favours for Stein, such as posting on his
spats and packing tea tablets bought on his account at the Army and Navy Store.2 Even "the
caves", the dingy basements of the British Museum,3 are better known than these people!

Stein mentions Miss Lorimer four times in the Reports concerning his Second and
Third Expeditions. In the Introduction to Serindia, the report of the Second Expedition,

The most important and urgent of those tasks [connected with the collection and the preparation of

Who was Miss Lorimer?
Florence Mary Glen Lorimer was born on December nth 1883 in the Free Church Manse
of Strathmartine, near Dundee. Her father, the Revd Robert Lorimer (1840-192S), was a
well-known minister of the Free Church of Scotland and author of a number of books.18
Her mother, Isabella Lockhart Lorimer (nee Robertson)19 (1849-1931), had a formidable
reputation: she was a strong woman, ambitious for her children and later renowned for her
work with the poor. She was so well-liked for helping people living in tenements to get
hospital treatment, that she was the only person able to walk unaccompanied by police
escort through the back streets of Dundee (when even the police only went in pairs).20
Miss Lorimer was educated at Dundee High School, then at Notting Hill High School,
and not only took up a place at Somerville College, Oxford, but did so as the Gilchrist
Scholar of 1902.21 She was one of only seventy four students in Somerville that year, when
the total number of women at Oxford University for the year 1902-3 was only 228, and
women were not considered as full members of the University. It was only eighteen years
later, in 1920, that women became members of the University; prior to 1920 they were
registered instead with the Association for Promoting the Higher Education of Women. It
was also in 1902 that Emmeline Pankhurst founded the Suffragette Movement.
Miss Lorimer left Oxford in 1905 with Hon. Moderations, Classics, Class 3 - a lower
degree than might have been expected from a scholar, but quite a common class of degree
for graduates of her year.22 She may have felt the pressures of her family's religious and
academic background. Her three older siblings had certainly lived up to their mother's
expectations. During Miss Lorimer's first year at Oxford, her oldest brother, Gordon (John
Gordon Lorimer [1870-1914]), was made a Commander [of the Order] of the Indian
Empire.23 During her second year, the second brother, Lock (David Lockhart Robertson
Lorimer [1876-1962]), was promoted to the Political Department of the Government of

In 1907, two years after graduation, Miss Lorimer was engaged by Mr E. B. Nicholson
(1882-1912) of the Bodleian Library, to work on the revision of the catalogue. Nicholson
was one of the first librarians to encourage the Bodleian to employ women, and she was
trained by Mr Strickland Gibson.29 She stayed at the Bodleian until 1909 when she
became, in her own words, "Assistant to Sir Aurel Stein".30 From the Stein papers, now
housed at the Bodleian, it is clear that Miss Lorimer was quite a find.

(Cardew's father was a Captain in the 74th Highlanders). Both came from successful
academic families, with close associations at Oxford. One of Cardew's sons, the potter
Michael Cardew (1901—83), when describing his own student days at Oxford wrote: "Did
I also give any weight to the reflection that for at least four generations some direct ancestor
had studied or taught at Oxford, starting in the eighteenth century with the semi-mythical
Dr Cornelius Cardew, D.D., of Liskeard and Truro?"89 There were also strong ties with
India. Miss Lorimer and her siblings were notable figures in that sphere, and one of
Cardew's sons was resident in India.90
Both Miss Lorimer and Mr Cardew had abandoned, apparently voluntarily, successful
careers and changed track completely. Miss Lorimer no longer worked with the celebrated
academics associated with Sir Aurel Stein and his collections. Mr Cardew had turned his
back on a legal career to go into the antiques trade, which is where, the family story goes,
he met Miss Lorimer. Their Oxford connections may have brought them together.
Miss Lorimer, now Mrs Cardew, went to live in Arthur Cardew's house in North View,
Wimbledon Common. It was the family house where Mr Cardew had lived with his first
wife Alexandra, their six children, a nanny and three maids, and was crammed with antique
furniture, china and music. Arthur Cardew was an amateur violinist who loved Corelli,
Haydn and Mozart beyond all other music,91 and one of his grandsons, Cornelius Cardew
(b. 1936), became a professional musician.
In 1931, M R Cardew's mother died in Bournemouth at the age of 81. Mrs Cardew's
period of ill-health at John Lewis in 1931 may have been associated with her mother's
death. Whether this is coincidence or not, the following year she left her job at the John
Lewis Partnership, and made another career move, this time away from the commercial
world back to the academic world.
On Monday 14th October 1932 she took over the duties of Assistant Librarian at the
Royal Asiatic Society, which was then preparing a Library Author Catalogue, funded by
the Carnegie United Kingdom Trust.92 By coincidence she joined the Royal Asiatic

Mrs Cardew died on February 15th 1967. She had been suffering from Parkinson's
Disease and had grown very bent in the back. She told her brother Bertie, who true to his
campaigning spirit insisted on visiting her at least once a year from Scotland, that it was
such a long way to come; she was content to sit in the chair by the window and watch the
clouds. A woman came daily to help her, but eventually she grew too ill, and needed more
care. Within days of moving out of her own home, she died of bronchopneumonia. Her
niece, Mrs Jean Dunlop, informed the Sutton Registrar that day. Sadly, once again,
"occupation unknown" was recorded for the family's much-loved Aunt Floss.110
But Miss Lorimer had several occupations during her lifetime, sometimes filling posts
traditionally held by men, and she earned the respect of the scholars she worked with. Her
contribution to the academic world remains in use today, though it is mostly regarded as
anonymous, in the form of Stein's Reports and the various library catalogues.
