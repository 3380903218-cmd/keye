As already pointed out in this chapter, the dominating role 
of textual sources in the digital humanities faces growing 
criticism, much as it does in digital history. Before having 
a closer look at visual and audio-visual materials in the 
next chapter, it is worth recalling that the supposed duality 
between textuality and visuality is quite misleading and 
should not be taken too strictly. Texts include images and 
vice versa. Today, Internet texts often include both sound 
and visuals. And, moreover, both texts and images can be 
considered as material remains of the past. Text is not an 
abstract order of characters but is always, to draw on Bruno 
Latour's idea, inscribed on material objects, on stone or 
marble, on paper or hard drives."' Texts are material entities 
that have dimensions: printed texts manifest themselves, for 
example, as folios and octavos, as broadsheets and tabloids. 
When considering texts as big data, metatext must seriously 
be taken into account, since it offers access to the material 
dimensions of the texts at hand. Metadata has recently been 
successfully used in the study of book history, in which it 
is vital to comprehend how books circulated as concrete 
artefacts in the past.101 In the future, these material ramifica-
tions might be avenues for bringing the quantifiable data of 
texts more closely together with the study of their contents 
and, thus, their impacts on the imaginations of readers.

Mapping and Viewing History 
In the early stages of digital history, in the 1990s, maps 
were of particular interest, perhaps more so than the textual 
materials discussed in the previous chapter, because large-
scale digitization projects had only recently begun. At the 
University of Virginia in 1993, William G. Thomas and 
Edward L. Ayers started a project on the US Civil War, 
entitled The Valley of the Shadow, that brought together the 
experiences of Confederate and Union soldiers. It published 
historical documents online, including letters, diaries, images, 
newspapers, church records, census records, tax records, 
statistics — and maps. The pre-war material, for example, 
included maps pertaining to geography, infrastructure, 
agriculture, politics, religion and slavery in the selected 
counties (Augusta in Virginia and Franklin in Pennsylvania). 
These were not merely digitized pre-existing visualizations 
but also an integral part of the project, produced for online 
use by the project members.' 
Visual sources have been at the heart of digital history 
from the start, and maps have been an essential ingre-
dient in visualizing archival data and establishing points for 
comparison. The following chapter continues by discussing 
maps further as sources and historical interpretations, fuelled 
by the so-called spatial turn. The chapter proceeds by 
selecting three other themes under discussion, the increased 
role of visual sources, including the idea of distant viewing,

Maps as digital sources 
Historians have always emphasized the meaning of space 
and spatiality. In the study of the past, where has been as 
important as when. This involves, for example, locations 
(such as cities and villages), natural landmarks (such as rivers, 
mountains or seas) and both natural and political borderlines. 
In addition to maps, spatiality has also been studied through 
an array of sources, from architectural plans and landscape 
images to verbal descriptions in travel accounts and fiction. 
Historians have also theorized on the significance of space, 
as demonstrated in Frederick Jackson Turner's famous essay 
`The significance of the frontier in American history' (1893) 
and Fernand Braudel's classic The Mediterranean and the 
Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II (1949). 
The so-called spatial turn in the humanities and social 
sciences had its roots in this enduring interest in spaces 
and places,' but in the 1990s it really entered the scholarly 
discourse. According to Doris Bachmann-Medick, this devel-
opment was inspired by the end of the Cold War at the 
turn of the 1980s and 1990s; with the end of the political 
polarity of Eastern and Western blocs, a need arose to map 
the reorganization of the global order, to go beyond previous 
security strategies and to understand how transnational flows 
would change the way the world was imagined.' Edward 
W. Soj''s Postmodern Geographies was a groundbreaking 
volume in 1989, followed by his Thirdspace in 1996. Soon, 
the spatial turn embraced several fields of study, including 
anthropology, archaeology, geography, gender studies and 
history.' For the digital historian, it is important to be aware 
of these backgrounds, but of course spatiality and its digital 
research methods are strongly linked to the development of 
geospatial information that has emerged at the same time as 
a result of multidisciplinary collaboration. 
The digitization of historical sources described in 
chapter 1 has also included maps. Today, many national

and international collections of digitized maps enable their 
study using both computational tools and more traditional

Since 2003, the library has offered rare maps through the 
Internet.' The National Library of Argentina now has 1,122 
downloadable historical maps on its website, and the digital 
collection features not only older maps but also a substantial 
amount of material from the twentieth century.' One of the 
largest current collections of historical maps, plans and views, 
comprising 4.5 million items, is located at the British Library. 
It includes not only scanned maps but also geospatial data, 
cartographic applications and digital aerial photographs, 
and it features both digitized and born-digital content.' The 
chronological range of the collection is over 2,000 years, 
and its size is suggested by the example of the King George 
III Topographical and Maritime collection, which alone 
includes `30-40,000 maps, plans and views, both printed 
and hand-drawn, of all parts of the world'. 9 Not all maps are 
two-dimensional, however, and the British Library also holds 
hundreds of terrestrial and celestial globes, which are three-
dimensional material artefacts.10
As sources, maps are layered cultural artefacts that include 
numbers, texts, symbols and images. Historical research 
draws on these sources, which can be objects of careful 
close reading and iconographic analysis or can be used for 
more distant reading via the computational study of visual 
concepts or textual content. Maps can be described as repre-
sentations of the physical world, but they also construct 
the world as we know it. Historical maps must always 
be understood in the context of the practices that origi-
nally contributed to their production, including the ways of 
measuring and representing distances and the strategies for 
aggregating data for maps; of course, the motives and uses 
of maps also influence the interpretation of these documents.

Maps possess a strong representational character as they 
usually try to capture the three-dimensional world in only 
two dimensions. In Exploring Big Historical Data, Shawn 
Graham, Ian Milligan and Scott Weingart point out that 
`these seemingly straightforward representations are loaded 
with difficult choices, as laying two-dimensional coordinates 
onto a 3D world means making complicated choices around 
what map projection to use'." They note, for example, that, 
in the Mercator projection that underlies Google Maps, 
Greenland looks almost as big as Africa, although Africa is, 
in reality, fourteen times larger.12
Present-day projections differ in how they address the 
problem of 2D/3D conversion, and many other factors influ-
enced how old maps projected the physical world. In the 
past, maps also represented a realm of imagination, and their 
environmental features, such as coastlines and riverbeds, 
may have transformed over time, so it is not always possible 
to reduce historical maps to the topographical world. The 
researcher must estimate the possibility of matching a 
historical map with a modern one and determine whether 
this serves the purpose of the scholarly task. If so, the next 
step is trying to identify the points on the historical map 
that correspond to latitudes and longitudes in the present-
day reference map (a process called georectification). Since 
the 1980s, geographical information systems (GIS) have 
provided tools for managing geographical information. GIS 
makes it possible to draw on historical maps by extracting 
their information and analysing them in comparison with 
other GIS data and data sets.13 The New York Public 
Library's collection of over 11,000 georectified historical 
maps.is openly accessible to users.14
Maps as historical interpretations 
The use of historical maps (and in fact any source) depends 
on the research problem. Sometimes, georectification is not 
an option, for example in the case when no actual historical 
map is available. Other possible sources of topographical 
and geographical information can be further processed 
into a digital map, presenting a historical interpretation. 
Mapping and Viewing History 61 
The historian Harri Kiiskinen drew on GIS in his study 
of the production and trade of terra sigillata pottery in 
Roman Etruria. To understand the trading routes, he tried to 
determine which rivers in Etruria were navigable at the time, 
2,000 years ago. In addition to archaeological and historical 
evidence, this work used a digital terrain model based on 
topographic data from NASA's Shuttle Radar Topography 
Mission. As a result, Kiiskinen was able to draw a map that 
reconstructed the river network in Etruria.15 Here, the digital 
map serves research purposes and expresses an important 
historical argument. 
Maps had already been used to visualize big historical data 
before the era of GIS and the digital humanities. An excellent 
reminder of this is the production of Charles-Joseph Minard 
(1781-1870), a French civil engineer who won fame for his 
informational graphics and maps.16 His most well-known 
map, from 1869, describes Napoleon's Russian campaign of 
1812. There is a geographic orientation in the image of the 
direction of the campaign, but the map incorporates many 
other elements of information, from the number of troops to 
the temperature." The map clearly shows how Napoleon's 
army suffered during its march towards' Moscow from the 
Russian border. Minard also captured phenomena of his own 
era, for example, migratory movements on a global scale. 
The nineteenth century was similar to today in that it was 
characterized by a sudden abundance of data provided by 
new technologies, such as the high-speed press, the railroad 
and the telegram." Today, Minard is often recognized as the 
developer of the so-called flow map; in 1845, spurred by the 
discussion of the development of railroad connections, he 
produced a map that showed how passengers circulated in 
the road routes between Dijon and Mulhouse.19
Nineteenth-century statistics have had a remarkable 
influence on how we visualize information. Graham, Milligan 
and Weingart mention the dot density map that was used, for 
example, by the English physician John Snow (1813-1858), 
who, drawing on statistical methods, revealed the correlation 
between contaminated water and cholera. Snow marked the 
locations of London cholera cases with a dot on a map and 
was able to show that many cases occurred around a water pump.2°

Although recognizing these roots is important, digital 
technologies obviously offer many new avenues for maps, 
mapping and data visualization in general. Digital maps 
enable zooming in and out so that they can function as 
platforms for accumulated data for various historical sources. 
For example, a city map can include textual information and

From visual evidence to distant viewing 
Thus far, this chapter has dealt with maps and mapping, 
which have been at the heart of digital history since the 
1990s. What must be discussed next is actual visual evidence 
from the past, including how it can contribute to and change 
our understanding of history. Today, digital historians have 
access to manuscript illustrations, photos, posters and works 
of art, but digitization rates vary in collections that sometimes 
offer free use and sometimes only restricted use. For many 
reasons, the boundary between free and restricted use also 
varies. For example, there may be copyright concerns that 
influence the availability of images. In museum collections, 
copyright holders are not only artists but also photographers 
who have produced digital reproductions. 
One of the most well-known examples of making visual 
content available is the Rijksmuseum in Amsterdam, which 
is a Dutch national museum that holds a collection of over 
a million objects, including 2,000 paintings. In 2012, the 
Rijksmuseum started an extraordinary project by making 
125,000 high-resolution images available and downloadable. 
The plan is to add 40,000 images annually until the entire 
collection is online. Users can explore these images through 
the museum's own Rijksstudio, make observations, take clips 
and use them both in research and for other purposes. At the 
time of writing (May 2020), there were as many as 676,693 
high-resolution images available through Rijksstudio.21 The 
Mapping and Viewing History 63 
collection is a substantial window, not only onto the Dutch 
past but also onto art history in general. 
Restricted collections can also be useful for research 
because their metadata are often available online. An 
excellent example is provided by RMN Photo. RMN refers 
to the French organization Reunion des musees nationaux, a 
joint enterprise of French art galleries and museums. Today, 
RMN Photo also includes foreign galleries, such as the 
Prado in Madrid and the National Galleries of Scotland in 
Edinburgh.22 The database includes small preview images that 
allow users to peek at the collection, and it is also possible to 
search by keyword. The collection is immense; a search of the 
keyword arbre (tree), returns over 22,000 images of various 
kinds of trees, including paintings, drawings, photographs, 
statues and many other kinds of objects from the Middle 
Ages to the present. This includes, for example, over 1,400 
paintings from the nineteenth century. 
In addition to RMN Photo and Amsterdam's Rijksmuseum, 
numerous national and international image collections can be 
put under the lens. National libraries around the world have 
digitized cultural artefacts and images, and many heritage 
projects offer visual content to users, from the American 
Memory project to Europeana. The latter, for example, 
contains transnational collections, such as First World War 
newsreels, postcards and letters. The film collection alone 
comprises as many as 2,726 items that can be regarded as 
one of the most comprehensive cinematic collections on the 
Great War.23 It is obvious that these collections set different 
premises and conditions for research than textual material, 
but they also offer many possibilities for digital historians.

Manovich and his team have processed massive collections 
of images and video, including 6,000 paintings by French

raphy collection of the Museum of Modern Art. From a 
historical perspective, the Impressionist collection of images 
is comprehensive, as it represents approximately 50 per cent 
of all paintings and pastels presented in the Impressionist 
exhibitions during the years 1874-1886. The experiment 
of the Software Studies Initiative started with the idea of 
extracting particular features — in fact, 200 of them from

actual aim of the text was to articulate basic concepts for the

average brightness and saturation, could offer to the digital 
analysis of large data sets of images. In 2019, Taylor Arnold 
and Lauren Tilton emphasized strongly what they call distant 
viewing: 
[W]e argue that DH [digital humanities] should consider, 
what we call, distant viewing — a methodological and 
theoretical framework for studying large collections of 
visual material. Distant viewing is distinguished from 
other approaches by making explicit the interpretive 
nature of extracting semantic metadata from images. 
In other words, one must `view' visual materials before 
studying them. Viewing, which we define as an interpretive 
action taken by either a person or a model, is necessitated 
by the way in which information is transferred in visual 
materials." 
Manovich did not use the expression `distant viewing', but 
this was exactly the aim of his texts: to try to track down 
what machine viewing could mean and how it could be used 
in the digital humanities. In recent years, `distant viewing' 
has been employed by other researchers as well,27 which 
shows the increasing interest in the computational study and 
cultural analytics of images. In the case of distant reading, the

question is: what does it mean when a machine `reads' a text, 
which features have to be computationally extracted and 
how could these features be used for analysis? The same goes 
for the analysis of images, both still and moving: what does

in their article: there is a `need for coding visual materials 
prior to exploratory data analysis of visual corpora'.' At the 
same time, it is important to recognize that there are different

Sound and vision 
Parallel to the previous discussion, in data science, and not 
always in connection to the digital humanities, much research 
has taken place in relation to sound and vision. Automatic 
speech recognition (ASR) is today a useful tool for the study 
of audio-visual material. It enables the extraction of speech 
and its convergence into text, which again can be analysed 
with text mining tools. Simultaneously ASR processing can 
be deployed for producing digital indexes of the contents of 
auditive or audio-visual sources and thus help to enhance 
existing metadata. 
Considering how much audio material is in the archives, 
computational methods could be employed to increase their 
accessibility and, of course, to study en masse sources that 
would otherwise be difficult to digest. As a research example, 
we could play with the idea of applying ASR to newsreels 
after the breakthrough of sound cinema in the late 1920s and 
early 1930s. Often, these newsreels were made with a silent 
camera, without any sound recorded on location. A voice-
over narration was added later in the studio. Newsreels were 
a text-driven form of audio-visual culture; therefore, speech 
extraction might offer illuminating results. In any case, the 
quality of sound, and possible background music, would 
make this research challenging. Another problem might 
be the fact that ASR draws heavily on language models in 
identifying words. It seems that there is insufficient research 
on historical language models, which from a historian's

long period, the analysis of movie content would give raw 
material, such as on how the environments, milieus and 
cultural artefacts used in these works change over time. 
In the study of audio-visual content, it is necessary to pay 
attention to the changing technologies after the nineteenth 
century: they influence the ways in which these cultural 
products can be used for digital analysis and how they can 
be disseminated. Early film history was preserved on nitrate 
cellulose material which was very fragile and flammable. 
Therefore, many of the early films have been lost, and their 
study must be based on other kinds of sources, including stills, 
manuscripts, newspaper ads and reviews. Acetate cellulose is 
more stable and became dominant after the Second World 
War. Film archives around the world have for decades aimed 
at preserving these copies, first by producing acetate dupli-
cates, now by making digital scans with 4k or even higher 
resolution. After the advent of video tape recorders in the 
1950s, many other technologies for audio-visual products

have been introduced. In the 1980s Betamax and VHS 
technology dominated consumer markets, but there were also 
other formats, such as Video 2000 and U-matic. In the 1990s, 
digital CDs, laserdiscs and DVDs came along, followed by 
Blu-ray discs and streaming services in the 2000s. In this 
time period resolutions also changed, from high definition 
(HD) to ultra-high definition (UHD) and from 2k to 4k or 
even 8k. The array of historical technologies is a challenge 
for the digital historian, interested in audio-visual content. 
Film archives have produced digital copies from these items 
but there are many problems to be solved, for example, in the 
use of home-made movies that can still be preserved in their 
original format. 
Visuality, textuality and metadata 
As a conclusion to this chapter, I would like to return to 
two other aspects in the study of visuality that should be 
remembered and remind us of the closeness to the study 
of textuality. The first issue is that the dominant part 
of digitization projects mentioned in this book is in fact 
based on images. Digitized books, journals and newspapers 
originate from image collections, as printed material too is 
first scanned into an image and only thereafter is the text 
recognized using OCR. This means, for instance, that all 
newspaper repositories are basically collections of images 
of newspapers, although this is only seldom highlighted, as 
most research methods emphasize texts to which OCR has 
been applied. Yet, there are many other opportunities in 
these resources. Textuality is not the opposite of visuality, 
but texts are visual entities in themselves and comprise 
colour, font type, font size, spacing and many other layout 
elements. The historical change of a front page, to name only 
one possible theme, could be explored based on the scanned 
material. How did headlines change over time? How many 
columns were there? When did drawings and photographs 
first appear on the cover? How is the role of advertisements 
transformed if analysed in a historical setting? In addition to 
actual scanned images, visuality can be approached through 
metadata. Usually, XML files include information-on the

These are standard details, but they give the option for the

databases consist of many more details. Let me continue with

and transcontinental coverage, not only of films made in the

Interdisciplinarity: 
Challenges for Research 
Vast resources are available for the digital historian. As the 
previous discussions show, at the same time as more material 
is easily available, there are many heuristic and source-critical 
aspects that have to be taken into consideration. A major 
challenge is the use of large data sets. Therefore, digital 
history often appears as interdisciplinary work and takes 
the form of collaborative research based on cooperation and 
joint work. There is a need for transgressing disciplinary 
borders not only because of the complex problems awaiting 
resolution but also because, in other fields of research, such 
generic tools have been, or might have been, developed 
and productively deployed that can be applied in other 
contexts. Digital historians think about their work in terms 
of their interest in historical problems, but already problem-
solving might require expertise from different disciplines and 
methodological traditions. It would be unfortunate not to 
take advantage of what has been found in other scientific 
communities and within different research paradigms. 
Digital history does not stand alone before this challenge. 
The world has always been complex, to be sure, but we are 
increasingly conscious of the global hazards we confront, 
which further emphasizes the need for interdisciplinary work. 
In his compelling book We Have Never Been Modern,
