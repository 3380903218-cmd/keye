Reading and Textuality in 
Digital History 
`Some books are to be tasted, others to be swallowed, 
and some few to be chewed and digested', wrote Francis 
Bacon in his classic essay, `Of studies', published in the late 
sixteenth century. He continued: `Some books are to be read 
only in parts; others to be read but not curiously; and some 
few to be read wholly, and with diligence and attention.' 
Bacon's famous words capture the multifaceted experience 
of reading, its different rhythms and functions, and also the 
fact that reading is always related to what, in the end, is 
being read and what are those texts that we encounter. For 
digital historians, the role of texts and textuality as well as 
the different practices of reading are essential. This chapter 
will first discuss reading as a research practice that is, and has 
been, vital for historians in their effort to understand the past. 
Reading is often entertaining and sometimes painful and 
laborious, but it is also central to our understanding of the 
world and our digestion of information. Historical research 
has often been characterized as a realm of books. Historians 
must engage in two dialogues at the same time; they must 
acquire knowledge from the sources of the past and converse 
with other historians, both past and present. Reading is a 
research practice. The same applies in the case of the digital 
historian, who simultaneously engages in the centuriet-old

Reading as a research method 
In his analysis on the history of reading, James Raven 
describes reading as a `notoriously problematic' exercise 
because the act of reading seems to eschew recording; `few 
write down what they do'. 2 Some might write comments 
in the margins or remarks in their notebooks; others might 
keep a reading diary. But still, the actual process remains 
enigmatic. What happens when a historian reads? In his 
Going to the Sources: A Guide to Historical Research and 
Writing, Anthony Brundage tried to make this process visible 
and stressed the need to read throughout one's research, not 
to defer from it but to engage in it right from the start.' There 
are also many kinds of reading habits, or registers of reading, 
that need to be activated. On the one hand, a scholar must be 
an effective reader, able to retain and process a lot of infor-
mation, but s/he must be a critical reader, too, to be able to 
filter out misleading or irrelevant information. Furthermore, 
a scholar must track down and verify many details, such as 
basic background information on the authors of primary 
sources, and to compare previous works of history to under-
stand the state of the art. 
Reading is a method of acquiring information - of `perusing 
written or printed matter' 4 — but it has wider ramifications 
because it involves interpretative work: efforts to identify 
similarities and differences, continuities and discontinuities, 
the testing of possible explanations and, ultimately, the 
drawing of conclusions. Rolf Engelsing, the German historian 
of reading, saw a conceptual distinction in the perusing of 
text, noting that extensive reading is needed when a larger 
overview of the material is required, and sometimes intensive 
reading is required when it is necessary to delve into the 
Reading and Textuality in Digital History 31 
details.' In the latter case, reading the text only once is insuf-
ficient; one has to read the same texts again and again to 
understand their underlying meanings and significant struc-
tures. Reading is never simply a matter of decoding textual 
signs. Rather, it involves one's memory of what has been read 
before, either in the same texts or previous ones. In addition 
to past experiences, reading also incorporates expectations 
about the future, since the now of reading continuously 
produces hypotheses about the possible pathways of forth-
coming developments in the work. 
Since the 1990s, historians have recurrently used the 
expression `close reading' to describe their method of 
reading.' Often, close reading refers to careful, intensive 
reading during which historians are close to the details while 
at the same time seeing larger contexts between the lines. The 
notion of `close reading' has become widespread in historical 
studies after the rise of the so-called new cultural history in 
the 1980s and especially 1990s.7 As a notion, close reading 
was borrowed from literary studies, in which its meaning 
was somewhat different. It was especially advocated by 
practitioners of the so-called New Criticism after the Second 
World War.' The new critics wanted to defer from cultural, 
historical and, especially, biographical contextualization and 
to interpret literary works as self-contained aesthetic objects. 
The idea behind close reading was to concentrate on the 
form, literary devices and internal structures employed in 
autonomous works of art. 9 It can be argued that, since 
the 1990s, close reading has — at least in the historians' 
discourse — emphasized careful examination of the form and 
expressions of the source but, in contrast to New Criticism, 
aimed at interpreting these structural aspects in the wider 
framework of cultural and historical change. 
The idea of close reading is related to the question of 
how texts make meaning. To be sure, the interest in textual 
signification has many backgrounds, but it was particularly 
inspired by semiotics and its interest in signs and signifiers 
in the 1960s and 1970s. As an expression, close reading is 
most commonly used to describe scholarly work on texts, 
hut in the semiotic understanding of culture, reading has 
meanings other than what can be encountered in written 
or printed texts in and of themselves. The semiotic turn

emphasized language in a more general way. We have natural 
languages, like Hindi, Korean and Portuguese, but semioti-
cians interpreted other means of symbolic communication 
as `languages', too. They argued, for example, that images 
can also be seen as texts with their own specific language. 
Sixteenth-century paintings were based on their own visual 
motifs, figures, ornaments and so on. Musical language, in 
turn, could consist of, say, melodies, chords and rhythmic 
units.' The general idea from the point of view of reading 
has been that we can read not only written or printed texts 
but many kinds of cultural products, and that reading refers 
to the interpretation and decoding of signs and symbols. 
The readers just have to know the different registers of this 
communication in the same way they know the natural 
language of the book, specifically its vocabulary and syntax. 
Reading with machines 
Today, in the middle of the rapidly changing media world, the 
word `reading' is increasingly used in cases where the reader is 
not actually a human being but a technological device. There 
are optical readers used, for example, in scanners to capture 
visual information and convert it into other formats so it can 
be further elaborated on computers. A barcode on the back 
cover of a book includes information about the product, 
and it can be optically read so that this information can be 
channelled to another system in order to use it on an invoice 
at the bookshop. Here, `reading' refers both to the decoding 
and transformation of data. Many of the digitization projects 
discussed in the previous chapter draw on the idea of optical 
reading. The vast collections of digital corpora have been 
produced using optical character recognition (OCR), which 
is the conversion of images into text. Recognition is based 
on the visual features of characters, and the essential element 
is that the text it produces is machine-readable, allowing 
a computerized analysis of the texts which would not be 
possible in the analogue form. The historical background 
of OCR is actually related to more conventional reading or 
the necessity to find new avenues for reading. Already in the 
early twentieth century, there was a need for technology that 
Reading and Textuality in Digital History 33 
could help to convert printed texts into a form that would 
not require human eyes. In 1913, Edmund Edward Fournier 
d'Albe developed his optophone, which scanned text and 
changed the characters into tones that enabled the blind to 
hear the letters and thus read without being able to see any 
of the characters." In subsequent decades, many technologies 
were developed to enhance the automatic recognition of 
characters.12
The digital humanities are based on the premise that data is 
machine-readable, meaning that information can be read not 
only by the researcher but also by a computer or software. 
An optical reader is only one device in the process of enabling 
machine-readability. If we define `reading' as both digesting 
and organizing information, machines can certainly read. If 
we further suggest that reading is interpretation as well, it 
is evident that machines can be taught to analyse and make 
interpretations of the data they have internalized. To further 
complicate the issue, in the present situation, we are not 
only readers ourselves, but we are also objects of reading in 
new and imaginative ways. The algorithmic `reading' of our 
words and deeds is an urgent issue. This means, too, that the 
cultural historical context of reading has profoundly changed 
over the last few decades. 
Distant reading 
The theme of this volume — digital history - is based on 
the current cultural condition in which we have big data, 
both digitized and born-digital, in machine-readable forms, 
which allows for the development of new methodologies 
for historical research and, unquestionably, new kinds of 
research settings and historical inquiries. If the nature, quality 
and extent of the source material available for research have 
changed, it is natural that the research toolbox must change 
in tandem. In this situation, the notion of close reading, 
cultivated extensively by historians and other researchers in 
the humanities for decades, has been challenged by the idea 
of distant reading in the 2000s. The term `distant reading' 
became popular as a polemic commentary on close rc.ading. 
It was coined by the literary scholar Franco Moretti in his

article `Conjectures on world literature', published in 2000 
in the New Left Review. Moretti argued that distant reading 
allowed us `to focus on units that are much smaller or much 
larger than the text: devices, themes, tropes — or genres and 
systems'.13 Instead of reading too closely, the reader had 
to get some distance to be able to see larger features that 
otherwise would not catch the eye as well as those smaller, 
subtle elements that gain weight only when they accumulate. 
In his 2005 book Graphs, Maps, Trees: Abstract Models for 
a Literary History, Moretti wrote that this distance `is not an 
obstacle, but a specific form of knowledge: fewer elements, 
hence a sharper sense of their overall interconnection. Shapes, 
relations, structures. Forms. Models.'" 
In his original critique in the New Left Review, Moretti 
provocatively presented close and distant reading as 
opposites. He even argued that the distance is `a condition 
of knowledge'.' 5 There are many grounds for this conclusion 
that will be discussed later in this chapter, as well as arguments 
that are not necessarily relevant for digital historians since, as 
I see it, the very ideas of closeness and its opposite, distance, 
can vary. There are many publications that try to tackle this 
dichotomy and go beyond the dualistic setting.16
The notion of distant reading is today employed frequently 
in the study of the digital humanities, and it is relevant for 
digital history too if the source material contains texts en 
masse. It has also meant the return of statistical analyses 
and quantitative methods after an era of more interpretive 
approaches in cultural studies. Often, distant reading is 
presented simply as a way of analysing `hundreds or thousands 
of books', or even as something that is more `objective' than 
close.reading." There are, however, several aspects that must 
be taken into consideration when interpreting Moretti. First, 
his approach arises from literary studies, in which there 
has always been plenty of discussion on different reading 
methods. As already argued, close reading as a practice was 
originally presented against the biographical interpretation of 
literary works, pointing out the importance of analysing the 
inner structures of texts and delving into the level of words 
and syntax. In literary studies, many other catchwords for 
reading have been emphasized. The notion of symptomatic 
reading, fuelled by psychoanalysis and Marxism, was based 
Reading and Textuality in Digital History 35 
on the assumption that the real meaning of a text lies in what 
it does not say, which means that the reader has to try to go 
beyond the surface and find hidden meanings. As an opposite, 
surface reading was introduced to take the face value of the 
text seriously and analyse `the complexity of literary surfaces' 
that had been `rendered invisible by symptomatic reading'." 
To be sure, Moretti underlines the study of numerous 
texts through digital methods. But, in applying his thoughts 
in historical studies, one must be conscious of the fact that 
his formulations of distant reading were, at least in their first 
iteration in the New Left Review, targeted against the literary 
canon. His point of departure was challenging the conven-
tional understanding of world literature. Moretti writes: 
What does it mean, studying world literature? How do we 
do it? I work on West European narrative between 1790 
and 1930, and already feel like a charlatan outside of 
Britain and France. World literature? 
Many people have read more and better than I have, of 
course, but still, we are talking of hundreds of languages 
and literatures here. Reading `more'. seems hardly to be 
the solution. Especially because we've just started redis-
covering what Margaret Cohen calls the `great unread'. 
`I work on West European narrative, etc. ...' Not really, I 
work on its canonical fraction, which is not even one per 
cent of published literature. And again, some people have 
read more, but the point is that there are thirty thousand 
nineteenth-century British novels out there, forty, fifty, 
sixty thousand — no one really knows, no one has read 
them, no one ever will.' 
Moretti was clearly inspired by Margaret Cohen's idea of the 
`great unread', presented in her 1999 book The Sentimental 
Education of the Novel. With this term, Cohen referred to 
that huge body of texts that had become unavailable or had 
been completely forgotten.20 This created a challenge for 
research. How can one know what these forgotten books 
really covered? What did they discuss? And why were they 
popular in their own time? One researcher could only work 
on a small segment of this vast collection of `unread' books.

This observation was in fact already an argument for trying 
to find new methodologies for studying literature. 
Moretti's aim was to advocate the idea of Weltliteratur, in 
the spirit of Goethe and Marx, 21 but with modern methods. 
As he argued, close reading `necessarily depends on an 
extremely small canon', since it is not possible to closely 
analyse as many texts as the understanding of world literature 
would require. Therefore, new methods are needed, and the 
researcher should `learn how not to read'. Furthermore, 
Moretti poignantly points out that `The United States is the 
country of close reading, so I don't expect this idea to be 
particularly popular.'22
It is obvious that Moretti's provocation was addressed 
in particular to literary scholars who analysed one single 
author or work and thus strengthened the existing canon. 
The choices of the researcher were not impartial but partici-
pated in constructing the notion of literature. It is important 
to note this when applying Moretti's ideas on distant reading 
in other fields of the humanities. Additionally, it should not 
be taken for granted that Moretti's representation of literary 
studies is doing justice to the array of different approaches 
and trends in his own field. Regardless, in historical studies, 
the relationship of the researcher to texts and textuality is 
clearly different from Moretti's image, since historians often 
tend to incorporate as much material as possible into their 
investigations, instead of concentrating on only a few texts. 
Remarkably, Moretti makes a reference to historians, 
too. He quotes Marc Bloch's slogan `Years of analysis for a 
day of synthesis'.23 and refers to the overarching approaches 
of Fernand Braudel and Immanuel Wallerstein. He points 
out that, in the end, the synthesized parts of, for example, 
Wallerstein's works are very condensed. The author crystal-
lized his `years of analysis' into `one third of a page'.24 This 
reveals Moretti's aim: he is advocating efforts to find new 
ways for creating abstractions based on massive amounts of 
data. For him, visualizations serve as a means to synthesize 
those observations made through computational methods. 
This is what he further developed in his book Graphs, Maps, 
Trees: Abstract Models for a Literary History.25

researcher should not be too close to the text: `Mlle more

ambitious the project, the greater must the distance be.i26 In 
the context of historical studies, this view can certainly be 
contested. It is essential to draw on big data, now available 
for research, and explore novel ways of observing this 
material from a distance, but at the same time, it is equally 
important to return from the distance back to the close 
encounter of individual texts and consider how the bigger 
picture might change our view of the details. This continuous 
motion between distance and closeness has been emphasized 
by many researchers since Moretti. 27 Scott Weingart has 
pointed out the moral aspect of the distance by arguing: 
`When you zoom out far enough, everything looks the same.' 
Therefore, it is necessary for researchers in the humanities 
`to bring our close reading perspectives to the distant view'.28
Frederic Clavert, in turn, has suggested that historians should 
`be able to perform a double reading (une double lecture)', 
to work in the archives and keep close relationships with 
sources via a human reading; at the same time, however, they 
should be able to look at everything from afar.29 Sometimes 
distancing from distant reading is necessary; it can give new 
insights into close reading, and vice versa. Tim Hitchcock, 
one of the pioneers of digital history and a developer of Old 
Bailey Online, a repository of London's Central Criminal 
Court, 1674-1913, has highlighted the importance of being 
able to scale between the macro and micro levels and empha-
sized the potential of `computer-assisted close reading'.30
In distant reading, the word `distant' has a twofold 
meaning: it refers to the effort of trying to refrain from 
delving into detail and keeping away from the textual surface 
of the source, and it also insinuates that the research data 
are so vast that they simply cannot be viewed but from a 
distance. Big data requires a distant approach since close 
reading is not possible — let us think back to those hundreds 
of millions of books that have been digitized. It would 
not be humanly possible to read them all, and they would 
perhaps remain as `the great unread' if only close reading 
was regarded as scholarly relevant. In this case, instead of 
humans, it is necessary to use machines as readers. If the 
big data is in machine-readable format, it can be organized 
through computational methods. But is this reading at all? If 
we define reading as needing a human consciousness that can

The challenge of big data 
For the digital historian, distant reading becomes an issue 
especially when assessing big data sets, often corpora of 
texts. Reading and interpretation of small amounts of texts, 
or even a singular text, is important, but it is still necessary 
to have a wider discussion on big data. Digital history could 
not have developed without large corpora of information. 
The discourse on big data began in the 1960s and 1970s 
with the expansion of electronic media and computerization. 
The Oxford Dictionary of English defines the concept as 
`data of a very large size, typically to the extent that its 
manipulation and management present significant logistical 
challenges; (also) the branch of computing involving such 
data'. 31 Interestingly, the Oxford Dictionary of English cites 
an early comment by a historian on big data that reveals the 
scepticism towards computer-aided history circa 1980.32 The 
comment comes from `The old new social history and the 
new old social history', an essay presented by the eminent 
American political scientist and historian Charles Tilly at 
the University of Michigan in October 1980. This reference 
shows that historians have always been interested in drawing 
on large accumulations of data. In his text, Tilly quotes at 
length his British colleague Lawrence Stone's critique of 
cliometrics, focusing particularly on the work of Robert 
Fogel and Stanley Engerman, who had used computers for 
social historical analysis.33 According to Stone, the cliome-
tricians `specialize[d] in the assembling of vast quantities of 
data by teams of assistants, the use of the electronic computer 
to process it all, and the application of highly sophisticated 
mathematical procedures to the results obtained'. 34 There 
Reading and Textuality in Digital History 39 
were severe problems, however, which were echoed by 
Tilly, who argued that `coding loses crucial details' and that 
`mathematical results are incomprehensible to the historians 
they are meant to persuade'. Furthermore, `the storage of 
evidence on computer tapes blocks the verification of conclu-
sions by other historians'. 35 Tilly continues: `None of the 
big questions has actually yielded to the bludgeoning of the 
big-data people.' He concludes with Stone's words that `in 
general the sophistication of the methodology has tended to 
exceed the reliability of the data, while the usefulness of the 
results seems — up to a point — to be in inverse correlation 
to the mathematical complexity of the methodology and the 
grandiose scale of data-collection'.' 
This reference in the Oxford Dictionary of English portrays 
an ambivalent vision of `big data' in the late 1970s and early 
1980s. It is important to remember that at that time, in 
addition to Fogel and Engerman's work, there were other 
computer-assisted projects; in Britain, for example, Edward 
A. Wrigley and Roger Schofield's study on the population 
history of England from the sixteenth to the nineteenth 
century,' and in Estonia, Juhan Kahk's several studies on 
rural population.' Computer-assisted history was particu-
larly characterized as a business for social historians. The 
reference in the Oxford Dictionary of English puts forward 
a critique by two renowned social historians whose criticisms 
have not yet been overcome. Today, doubts regarding the 
interdisciplinary nature of digital history remain. How does 
one ensure that everything is processed correctly? How does 
one ensure source criticism has been taken seriously? How 
does one present results in a manner that is understandable 
and relevant to both historians and computer scientists? 
And how does one give future generations the possibility of 
verifying the results?

Internet servers or individual mobile phones. There are, as

and/or high-variety information assets that demand cost-

Texts for historical research 
In historical studies, there is a long tradition of analysing the 
textual remains of the past, both manuscripts and printed 
texts. When academic historical research really started in the 
nineteenth century, epitomized by such historians as Leopold 
von Ranke in Germany, textual sources were highly valued, 
Reading and Textuality in Digital History 43 
and not only textual but archival sources in particular." 
According to that view, there would be no history if recorded 
sources had not been available. The French historians 
Charles-Victor Langlois and Charles Seignobos expressed 
this principle famously in their 1898 Introduction aux etudes 
historiques, translated into English in 1904 as Introduction 
to the Study of History: `No document, no history.'' 
In fact, the early days of academic history produced a 
kind of hierarchy of textuality that has had an impact on the 
concept of history: archival official documents were ranked 
above private correspondence, and handwritten texts were 
ranked above printed and more popular publications. In that 
sense, textuality was never regarded as one genre but as an 
overarching category for many kinds of cultural products. 
Fuelled by their passionate devotion to texts, historians 
developed their skills in source criticism and hermeneutic 
interpretation. Towards the end of the nineteenth century, 
however, and increasingly in the early twentieth century, 
historians were criticized for being too dependent on written 
and printed sources and failing to acknowledge other traces of 
the past. Such pioneers as the Dutch historian Johan Huizinga 
emphasized visual sources in his 1919 Waning of the Middle 
Ages, which starts with a look at the enigmatic paintings by 
the Van Eyck brothers.46 To be sure, the Middle Ages could 
hardly be understood without visual material. From the 1920s 
onwards, historians from the French Annales School, for 
example, suggested that any kind of cultural product could 
be a source of information for a historian, not only written 
and visual sources but also oral tradition, material remains, 
buildings, statistics and audio-visual sources.47 From this 
perspective, the discussion in the digital humanities seems 
sometimes like a return to those similar views that were 
debated 100 years ago: texts, especially printed ones, are 
easy to access, and there is already a methodology for their 
successful employment in research. But this is, of course, only 
partially true, since the context is different today. One of the 
reasons for the emphasis on textual big data today is the fact 
that historical linguistics has had a long tradition of studying 
digitized corpora; researchers of this field were doing so long 
before such corpora were found by historians and other 
humanities researchers.

been produced by national libraries or through private initia-

Newspapers are probably the most common form of 
digitized source material for historians today. It might be 
argued that, as early as the nineteenth century, newspapers 
were a form of big data avant la lettre. Since the 1830s, high-
speed presses have allowed the inexpensive printing of tens 
of thousands of newspapers in a day. The English politician 
and author Sir Edward Bulwer Lytton described newspapers 
as `the chronicle of civilization, the common reservoir into 
which every stream pours its living waters, and at which 
every man may come and drink'. 48 There was newspaper 
publishing, of course, since the seventeenth century, and these 
early papers are important for digital historians, too, but 
since the beginning of the nineteenth century, the periodical 
press formed a network that reached all corners of the world. 
Because of that, they offer a basis for large-scale analyses, a 
basis that transgresses continental borders. 
Over the last fifteen or twenty years, national libraries 
have digitized their newspaper collections. If we add up the 
digitized papers of, for example, Australia, Finland, Germany, 
Mexico, New Zealand, the Netherlands, the United Kingdom 
and the United States, we get over 100 million pages of 
content. There is no search engine that would enable the 
inquiry of all these collections with only one search, and 
researchers must consult each of these repositories separately. 
There are many curated lists of historical newspaper 
archives that help researchers navigate globally and find 
relevant collections. One of the most comprehensive lists can 
be found on Wikipedia, which includes digitized collections 
from countries like Argentina, Brazil, Canada, Equatorial 
Guinea, Iceland, Japan, Russia and Slovenia.49 Self-evidently, 
there is a lot of variation in these collections, both in terms of 
accessibility and search options. Some collections are behind

The search interfaces of digitized text collections are 
based on optical character recognition. The methods to 
capture text from scanned images have been developed 
since the late nineteenth century, but they became practical 
research tools from the 1970s onwards.S8 One of the most 
successful OCR programs is the ABBYY FineReader, released 
in 1993. The latest version of the software can recognize 192 
different languages.59 The development has been tremendous, 
but OCR still creates a problem for historical research, 
since the further back in time we go, the more difficult 
character recognition seems to be, because of the instability 
of font shapes, the differences in original printing materials 
and the multi-layered scanning history of a given source, 
including that so much has been digitized from microfilm. 
Tim Hitchcock has pointed out that OCR `does not produce 
reliable results when applied to early modern publications, 
the cheap and quotidian print of the nineteenth century, 
or any form of complex formatting including tables, lists 
and advertisements'.60 Furthermore, the quality does not 
vary historically, in the sense that older material would be 
automatically poorer than modern material. For example, 
in the late nineteenth century there were often six, seven or

even eight columns per page, and although the paper size 
increased, the font size kept diminishing, which again makes 
it difficult for the OCR program to correctly recognize the 
text. Typesets also present a problem: current software 
can more easily recognize modern fonts than the older 
Gothic typeset that was popular before the twentieth century. 
The OCR methods are, however, becoming better for both 
historical and non-European sources, and new solutions will 
soon be in the toolbox. For example, The Early Modern 
OCR Project (eMOP) at the Texas A&M University offers 
an excellent example of the imaginative ways of combining 
open source OCR software and book history to improve the 
accuracy of OCR for early modern printed texts.61

Distant reading techniques 
As described above, there are numerous textual corpora that 
digital historians can employ. Let me return to the question 
of reading. What does reading mean, and what would it 
entail, if the researcher has to adopt millions of words, or 
even more. How can interpretation be realized in such a case? 
Reading has always involved interpretation and this is 
the case also in distant reading, which is not against human 
interpretations. Rather, it can merely be argued that distant 
reading divides reading into different processes: the recog-
nition of text, its organization and its structural analysis, 
for example, can be done by a machine reader and the inter-
pretation of this process by a human reader, specifically, the 
researcher. The process of human interpretation can also be 
computer-aided or run by artificial intelligence. 
In the present-day digital humanities, and digital history, 
distant reading does not refer to any single way of reading 
or any coherent set of methodologies. Many solutions for 
organizing big data have been developed during recent 
decades. They have not been invented as responses to the idea 
of distant reading, since some of them have longer histories, 
but they have clearly gained more weight in the toolboxes 
used by humanities researchers as ways of coming to terms 
with large data collections. 
Topic modelling is a statistical method for studying under-
lying structures in a series of documents.78 `Topic', in this 
case, refers to a group of words that tends to occur in the 
data. Modelling these topics is an open-ended process, as the 
amount of topics is ultimately a result of how the researcher 
seeds the algorithm.79 The most common statistical model is 
latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA), which was developed in 
2002 by David Blei, Andrew Ng and Michael I. Jordan. 80
There are also easy-to-use software programs, like Mallet, 
for topic modelling.81 The main idea is that the more data 
researchers have, the more useful topic-modelling tools are 
Reading and Textuality in Digital History 53 
for organizing that data, finding which words tend to cluster 
together and identifying topics. Topic modelling is useful 
for unstructured text in which there are no annotations that 
would help the computer interpret semantic meaning. It 
is important not to mix these topics with themes or to see 
the two as identical. For example, a text might have a fixed 
number of themes that can be observed through careful close 
reading. In topic modelling, however, the algorithm finds 
as many topics as the researcher has given in the settings 
of the program. If the number is too high, the topics might 
be dispersed, which makes their semantic interpretation 
difficult. In practice, the researcher must try to find the most 
fruitful number of topics for the analysis.' 
There are many examples of the use of topic modelling 
in historical research. In 2011, for example, Tze-I Yang, 
Andrew J. Torget and Rada Mihalcea experimented with 
topic modelling by using historical newspapers as a data 
set. They analysed papers published in Texas from 1829 
to 2008, reviewing over 230,000 pages, which is already a 
big collection of data. They divided the material into four 
subgroups for testing topic modelling and pre-processed the 
data before analysis. In this process, so-called stop words 
also had to be identified. These stop words are commonly 
used words, like pronouns, that must be removed before 
running the algorithm so as to make the results as fruitful 
as possible. Finally, the researchers were able to extract 
historically meaningful topics from a large amount of data on 
Texan history, but they also confronted the problem of noise 
in the optical character recognition.83 If the OCR is bad, it 
means that there are so many broken or misspelled words 
that the results become confused. Despite this, the study, and 
many other case studies,84 revealed the potential of statistical 
analyses such as topic modelling in helping to organize data 
and inject semantic meaning. 
Topic modelling is only one example of distant reading. 
Moretti himself emphasized visual techniques, or as he put it, 
`graphs, maps and trees'. With graphs, he wanted to visualize 
the changes in the genre of historical novels with maps to 
illustrate the geographical aspects of fiction and with trees 
to show how different types of stories could be classified." 
In 2015, in their effort to classify different close and distant

One of the most vibrant areas of distant reading is the 
study of text reuse, which can include exact quotations but 
can also cover intentional or unintentional borrowing and 
paraphrasing.92 Repeated and copied passages can, of course, 
be identified through close reading, which theologists, histo-

have done for centuries. The larger the amount of text, the 
more beneficial the use of digital methodologies is. Today, it 
is possible to detect similarities in vast corpora, such as the 
data dumps of millions of newspaper pages. Here, distant 
reading helps to algorithmically recognize relations within 
the corpora, which again allows the researcher to close read, 
for example, chains of repeated media content. Algorithmic 
solutions can be based on detecting similar character strings, 
with tolerance to OCR errors and other forms of noise, or 
locating sequences of words and how words occur together.

The culture of copying has many historical ramifications 
and can shed interesting light on the repetitive nature of the 
past. This can only become visible from a distance. In a text 
reuse project from Finland, it was possible to mine all the 
published issues of newspapers and journals from the birth 
of the local newspaper business until 1920 - in sum, 5.1 
million pages. In the end, 13.8 million clusters of reused texts 
were found. Most of the cases of content-sharing happened 
within one year, but over two million clusters lasted longer. 
Astonishingly, there were also strikingly slow processes of 
repetition; the longest reuse cases were almost as long as the 
time span of the project." This long-term perspective shows 
the benefits of computational methods.

considered as material remains of the past. Text is not an 
abstract order of characters but is always, to draw on Bruno

successfully used in the study of book history, in which it

artefacts in the past.101 In the future, these material ramifica-

texts more closely together with the study of their contents 
and, thus, their impacts on the imaginations of readers.

Mapping and Viewing History 
In the early stages of digital history, in the 1990s, maps 
were of particular interest, perhaps more so than the textual 
materials discussed in the previous chapter, because large-
scale digitization projects had only recently begun. At the 
University of Virginia in 1993, William G. Thomas and 
Edward L. Ayers started a project on the US Civil War, 
entitled The Valley of the Shadow, that brought together the 
experiences of Confederate and Union soldiers. It published 
historical documents online, including letters, diaries, images, 
newspapers, church records, census records, tax records, 
statistics — and maps. The pre-war material, for example, 
included maps pertaining to geography, infrastructure, 
agriculture, politics, religion and slavery in the selected 
counties (Augusta in Virginia and Franklin in Pennsylvania). 
These were not merely digitized pre-existing visualizations 
but also an integral part of the project, produced for online 
use by the project members.' 
Visual sources have been at the heart of digital history 
from the start, and maps have been an essential ingre-
dient in visualizing archival data and establishing points for 
comparison. The following chapter continues by discussing 
maps further as sources and historical interpretations, fuelled 
by the so-called spatial turn. The chapter proceeds by 
selecting three other themes under discussion, the increased 
role of visual sources, including the idea of distant viewing,
